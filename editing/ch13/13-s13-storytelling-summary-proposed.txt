## Storytelling {#sec-generalized-poisson-storytelling}

The **storytelling stage** translates the full Generalized Poisson workflow into a message for an applied audience. Imagine presenting to marine biologists studying horseshoe crab reproduction. They care about two distinct things, and the story keeps them separate:

- **Inferential**: which female characteristics are associated with the expected number of satellite males, and how certain are we?
- **Predictive**: do those characteristics help predict satellite counts for crabs we have not measured?

![Image by [*Manfred Steger*](https://pixabay.com/users/manfredsteger-1848497/) via [*Pixabay*](https://pixabay.com/vectors/pixel-cells-thread-needle-diy-sew-6230155/).](img/thread.png){width="350"}

The journey to the answer matters as much as the answer. Chapter 10's Classical Poisson model failed these data: the conditional variance was roughly three times the mean, expected zeros were less than a third of what was observed, and its residual deviance was far above its degrees of freedom. This chapter's candidate — Generalized Poisson regression — adds exactly one parameter aimed at exactly those failures. The training-stage audit showed the repair working: the Pearson statistic with the GP variance function is in line with its degrees of freedom, the expected zero count moved from `r round(expected_zeros_poisson, 1)` to `r round(expected_zeros_gp, 1)` (against `r observed_zeros` observed), and the likelihood-ratio test formalized the need for the dispersion parameter.

From the inferential point of view, the motivating question was:

> **How is the expected number of satellite males associated with female crab width, weight, colour, and spine condition?**

Two findings carry the answer. First, the **dispersion itself is a finding**: on the testing-set refit, $\hat\theta = `r round(theta_hat_test, 2)`$ per @tbl-generalized-poisson-test-refit-r, the counts vary about $`r round(1 / (1 - theta_hat_test)^2, 1)`$ times as much as a Poisson model with the same mean would allow, and the test of $H_0: \theta = 0$ is decisive ($p \approx `r formatC(p_theta_test, format = "e", digits = 1)`$). Any scientific statement about these crabs that ignores this dispersion is built on an unsound uncertainty floor.

Second, within the proper uncertainty the evidence concentrates on **colour**. Relative to `Light` females, `Dark` females are estimated to have `r round(100 * (1 - exp(cs_test["colorDark", "Estimate"])))`% fewer expected satellite males (95% interval for the ratio from `r round(exp(cs_test["colorDark", "Estimate"] - 1.96 * cs_test["colorDark", "Std. Error"]), 2)` to `r round(exp(cs_test["colorDark", "Estimate"] + 1.96 * cs_test["colorDark", "Std. Error"]), 2)`), and `Medium dark` points the same way with a wider interval. The body-size variables that dominate the raw plots do not carry a statistically decisive adjusted effect at this sample size — an honest disappointment that the GP model states plainly precisely *because* its standard errors are calibrated. Stakeholders should hear that sentence whole: *the association story is about colour; the size-gradient visible in exploratory plots is not, at these sample sizes, distinguishable from chance under a model whose uncertainty we trust.* Chapter 10's Poisson-based refit read slightly more generously [@sec-classical-poisson-storytelling]; the difference is not new data but correctly sized intervals.

From the predictive point of view, the motivating question was:

> **How well can the available regressors be used to predict the expected number of satellite males for a female crab?**

Plainly: the regressors are not reliable point predictors on held-out crabs. The extended GP model's held-out MAE is `r round(gp_prediction_metrics_table_r["Extended GP model", "MAE"], 2)` satellites against `r round(gp_prediction_metrics_table_r["Training-mean baseline", "MAE"], 2)` for a rule that always predicts the training average of `r round(mu_baseline_r, 2)`; RMSE tells the same story (`r round(gp_prediction_metrics_table_r["Extended GP model", "RMSE"], 2)` against `r round(gp_prediction_metrics_table_r["Training-mean baseline", "RMSE"], 3)`). Only the distribution-aware negative log predictive mass tilts marginally toward the extended model (`r round(gp_prediction_metrics_table_r["Extended GP model", "Mean negative log predictive mass"], 3)` against `r round(gp_prediction_metrics_table_r["Training-mean baseline", "Mean negative log predictive mass"], 3)`), reflecting the better-calibrated spread rather than better locations.

The responsible story, then, is not "*the model explains satellite counts*". It is:

> *Satellite counts are strongly overdispersed — a stable, tested property of these data, present on data the model never saw. After adjusting honestly for this dispersion, the clearest association is darker colour with fewer satellites; the observed body-size gradient is compatible with a wide range of adjusted effects, including none. Used predictively, the crab characteristics give an honest picture of how uncertain any single crab's count remains, rather than an accurate point forecast.*

That is a usable scientific message, and it is only available because the model was allowed to say how variable the world is. The [Negative Binomial](#sec-negative-binomial) and [Zero-Inflated Poisson](#sec-zero-inflated-poisson) chapters show two alternative routes to overdispersion and zero surplus (variance *quadratic* in the mean; a two-component zero process); the exercises compare them directly against what was done here.

## Chapter Summary {#sec-chapter-13-summary}

This chapter presented **Generalized Poisson (GP) regression** as the book's answer to count data that violate equidispersion **in either direction**. The running horseshoe crab case study framed the two guiding inquiries summarized in @tbl-generalized-poisson-chapter-summary-inquiries.

| Inquiry type | Guiding question | Main takeaway |
|:---:|:---:|:---|
| **Inferential inquiry** | How is the expected number of satellite males associated with female crab width, weight, colour, and spine condition? | Dispersion is decisively present ($\hat\theta \approx 0.51$ on the testing refit, variance about $4.2\times$ the mean, $H_0: \theta = 0$ rejected overwhelmingly); the `Dark` vs `Light` colour contrast is the clearest adjusted association; body-size effects do not clear GP-calibrated uncertainty at this sample size. |
| **Predictive inquiry** | How well can the available regressors be used to predict the expected number of satellite males for a female crab? | Held-out point prediction does not beat a training-mean baseline (MAE 2.53 vs 2.50); the GP model's contribution is calibrated predictive spread, not lower error. |

: Summary of the two guiding inquiries in the Generalized Poisson regression case study. {#tbl-generalized-poisson-chapter-summary-inquiries .striped .hover}

The model itself has three parts (@sec-generalized-poisson-data-modelling): a **random component** $Y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i \sim \operatorname{GPO}(\lambda_i, \theta)$ with mean $\mu_i = \lambda_i/(1-\theta)$ and variance $\mu_i / (1-\theta)^2$; the **systematic component** $\eta_i = \mathbf{x}_i^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}$; and the **log link** $\log \mu_i = \eta_i$, which ties the rate-like parameter to the regressors through $\lambda_i = (1-\theta)\exp(\eta_i)$. The dispersion parameter $\theta \in (-1, 1)$ carries the meaning: $\theta = 0$ nests the Classical Poisson model as an *interior* point, $\theta > 0$ inflates the variance above the mean, and $\theta < 0$ compresses it below the mean. The Chapter 13 distributional summary in @sec-generalized-poisson-distribution collects the distribution's properties independently of any regression.

Estimation is **joint maximum likelihood** in $(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \theta)$ — there is no iteratively reweighted least squares shortcut as in a classical GLM — and uncertainty comes from the inverse information matrix, with the expected-versus-observed information difference explaining small `R`/`Python` discrepancies (@sec-generalized-poisson-estimation). In `R`, {VGAM}'s `vglm()` with `genpoisson1(zero = "dispind")` fits the model and we decoded its dispersion row back into $\theta$; in `Python`, the chapter's own `GenericLikelihoodModel` subclass maximizes the log-likelihood directly, with the logistic map keeping $\theta$ in $(-1, 1)$.

Goodness of fit ran as a full training-stage audit (@sec-generalized-poisson-gof): the likelihood-ratio test of $\theta = 0$ (classical $\chi^2_1$ applies because $0$ is interior), the Pearson statistic standardized by the GP variance function, the observed-versus-fitted comparison, the residual deviance with its small-counts approximation caveat, and the GP zero-count check $\sum_i \exp(-\hat\lambda_i)$ — the check that flagged the remaining zero surplus as the feature a dispersion parameter cannot repair on its own.

Interpretation kept Chapter 10's grammar (@sec-generalized-poisson-interpretation): coefficients are additive on the log expected-count scale and multiplicative as **expected-count ratios**, categorical coefficients compare against reference categories, and $\theta$ was given its own reporting convention (parameter plus its implied variance multiplier).

The results stage delivered the conclusions on held-out data (@sec-generalized-poisson-results): inference from a testing-set refit, prediction from the training-fitted model, six executed metrics against an intercept-only GP baseline, and an honest answer to both inquiries.

![Image by [*Manfred Steger*](https://pixabay.com/users/manfredsteger-1848497/) via [*Pixabay*](https://pixabay.com/vectors/pixel-cells-emotion-confused-6230199/).](img/aha.png){width="300"}

Where does this model sit among the book's count-model tools? **Negative Binomial** regression (@sec-negative-binomial) models overdispersion with a variance quadratic in the mean, and **Zero-Inflated Poisson** regression (@sec-zero-inflated-poisson) models a structural zero surplus with a mixture; both are one-direction tools. The Generalized Poisson model's distinguishing feature is the sign of one scalar: it treats overdispersion and underdispersion symmetrically, which is why this book places it as the capstone of the mean-variance-flexible count models. The 99 B-Line coding case at the end of the chapter exercises the underdispersed side of the same machinery — where the Classical Poisson model errs in the *opposite* direction — and the practice exercises develop the conceptual material (the dispersion parameter's geometry, comparison with the sibling models, and the dispersion ratio as decision quantity).
