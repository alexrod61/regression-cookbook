::: {.CodingCase}
:::: {.CodingCase-header}
99 B-Line Bus Arrivals During Rush Hour
::::
:::: {.CodingCase-container}
A regional transit authority is reviewing the reliability of its busiest express corridor — the **99 B-Line**, which runs along Broadway between Commercial–Broadway Station and the University of British Columbia. During weekday rush hours, the corridor operates on a **tight schedule**: a bus is planned to arrive at very short, nearly constant headways, and dispatchers intervene when gaps become too large.

For a service-planning review, analysts counted, for each of 200 weekday rush-hour windows, how many buses **actually arrived** at a central stop in one hour (`buses_per_hour`), together with the day of the week (`day_of_week`), whether it was raining during the window (`is_raining`), the number of buses scheduled for that window (`scheduled_frequency`), and a time-window index (`time_window`).

![Image by [*manfredsteger*](https://pixabay.com/users/manfredsteger-1848497/) via [*Pixabay*](https://pixabay.com/vectors/pixel-cells-traffic-lights-police-6230164/).](img/traffic-lights.png){width="475"}

Because buses are dispatched on an enforced schedule, rush-hour arrivals are expected to be **more regular than random**: under a homogeneous Poisson process, hourly counts fluctuate with variance equal to the mean, whereas a well-controlled service pins counts close to the scheduled target. In the language of this chapter, the working hypothesis is **underdispersion**, that is, $\theta < 0$ in the Generalized Poisson model. Since underdispersed fits cannot be obtained from {VGAM}'s `vglm()` (its dispersion index $(1-\theta)^2 > 1$ cannot drop below one), this exercise fits the model by **direct numerical likelihood maximization** in both languages, applying the log-likelihood machinery derived in @sec-generalized-poisson-estimation.

You will reproduce the complete modelling workflow developed in this chapter:

1. Formulate the **statistical questions**.
2. Understand the **study design** and **variables**.
3. Wrangle the data.
4. Explore the **training data**.
5. Fit and interpret a simple Generalized Poisson regression model.
6. Assess **goodness of fit**.
7. Extend the model with additional regressors.
8. Evaluate **held-out prediction**.
9. Obtain **inferential summaries** from a **testing-set refit**.
10. Communicate the findings in **plain language**.

@tbl-generalized-poisson-bline-variables summarizes the variables in this dataset.

| Variable | Description |
|:---:|:---:|
| `buses_per_hour` | Number of buses that actually arrived during the one-hour rush-hour window. |
| `day_of_week` | Day of the week of the observation: Monday through Friday. |
| `is_raining` | Rain indicator: `0` for no rain during the window and `1` for rain. |
| `scheduled_frequency` | Number of buses the timetable schedules for that window. |
| `time_window` | Sequential index of the observed window in the study period. |

: Variables used in the 99 B-Line Generalized Poisson regression exercise. {#tbl-generalized-poisson-bline-variables .striped .hover}

The response `buses_per_hour` is a count of service deliveries under dispatch control. The regressors `day_of_week` and `is_raining` describe conditions that may be associated with achievable service levels: traffic and boarding times vary by day, and rain slows loading and traffic. The two remaining columns are **design information rather than conditions**: `time_window` only indexes the observations, and `scheduled_frequency` is set by the authority — its variation across windows is tiny (17 to 21 scheduled buses) precisely because rush-hour dispatch is tightly controlled. The modelling will keep both of these columns out of the regressor set.

Finally, because this is an observational record rather than an experiment, associations between conditions (for instance, rain) and arrivals should not be read as causal effects: rain may be a marker for heavier traffic months, construction periods, or seasonal schedules. The purpose of the exercise is to practice Generalized Poisson regression on an **underdispersed** count response, while keeping the limits of the data and model visible.

Question 13.15 — Nature of the Response
::::
::::{.Question-container}
The response `buses_per_hour` ranges from `12` to `30` with **no zero counts**, whereas the horseshoe-crab response had many zeros. Does the absence of zeros conflict with the Generalized Poisson model's support, and what does the observed range suggest about the data-generating process compared with the crab data?

> **Hint:** Check what the GP probability mass function assigns at $y = 0$ when the mean is near 20.
::::
:::

::: {.Answer}
::::{.Answer-header}
Answer 13.15
::::
::::{.Answer-container}
<details>
<summary><strong>Click here to reveal the answer!</strong></summary>

There is **no conflict**. The GP support $\{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$ merely *allows* zero; it does not *require* zeros. With $\mu \approx 20$ and $\theta < 0$, the zero-count probability $\exp(-\lambda) = \exp(-(1-\theta)\mu)$ is astronomically small (of order $\exp(-25) \approx 10^{-11}$), so observing zero in any window is essentially impossible. The tight range $12$–$30$ around a mean near 19 with variance far below the mean is the fingerprint of a **scheduled, controlled process** — the opposite regime from the crab data, where occupancy was uncertain and variance exceeded the mean. The chapter's dispersion parameter $\theta$ is precisely what adapts the same model class to both regimes.

</details>

Question 13.16 — Baseline Category and Excluded Columns
::::
::::{.Question-container}
Why is `Monday` chosen as the reference level for `day_of_week`, and why are `scheduled_frequency` and `time_window` excluded from the regressor set even though they are numeric and complete?

> **Hint:** Think about interpretability of contrasts for the first part, and about what a regressor *is* (a measured condition vs. a design constant or identifier) for the second.
::::
:::

::: {.Answer}
::::{.Answer-header}
Answer 13.16
::::
::::{.Answer-container}
<details>
<summary><strong>Click here to reveal the answer!</strong></summary>

**Baseline.** Monday opens the operating week and is the natural anchor for schedule performance after the weekend; making it the reference level means each weekday coefficient reads as "relative to Monday," which is how the service review is framed. Any other level would only reparameterize the same contrasts (see Question 13.12).

**Exclusions.** A regressor must describe a *condition* of the observation that could plausibly be associated with the response. `time_window` is an identifier — its "association" with the response would be a machine-generated artifact, not a property of the corridor. `scheduled_frequency` is a nearly constant design setting (17–21 buses) imposed by the authority; it is not a condition the service experiences, and within this dataset its narrow variation cannot be separated from the intercept. Neither belongs in the mean model.

</details>

Based only on the EDA, what would you predict about the sign and approximate magnitude of $\hat\theta$ under (i) an intercept-only mean and (ii) the mean model with rain and weekday contrasts? Which of the two should show the *larger* $|\hat\theta|$, and why?

Answer 13.17
::::
::::{.Answer-container}
<details>
<summary><strong>Click here to reveal the answer!</strong></summary>

Both fits must find $\hat\theta < 0$: every ratio seen so far is below one, and $1/(1-\theta)^2 < 1$ requires $\theta < 0$.

(i) Intercept-only: ratio $\approx 0.75$ implies $1-\hat\theta \approx 1/\sqrt{0.75} \approx 1.155$, so $\hat\theta \approx -0.15$.

(ii) With rain and weekday contrasts: the group-wise ratios (0.543–0.838) are *lower* than the overall ratio, because part of the overall variance was day-to-day location variation. Conditioning absorbs that part, so the residual ratio should drop — meaning $\hat\theta$ should move **further** from zero, on the order of $-0.25$ to $-0.30$.

The simple-model and extended-model stages below will confirm both predictions directly.

#### Simple Generalized Poisson Regression and Goodness of Fit {#sec-generalized-poisson-bline-simple-model}

The simple model conditions the arrival counts on rain only:

$$
Y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i \sim \operatorname{GPO}(\lambda_i, \theta), \qquad
\lambda_i = (1 - \theta)\exp(\beta_0 + \beta_1 \texttt{is\_raining}_i),
$$

so that $\exp(\hat\beta_1)$ is the expected-count ratio of rainy versus dry rush-hour windows, and $\hat\theta$ summarizes residual departure from equidispersion. Because $\hat\theta$ is expected to be **negative** here — outside what {VGAM}'s dispersion-index parameterization can reach — the fit is obtained by **direct numerical maximization** of the joint log-likelihood derived in @sec-generalized-poisson-estimation.

#| echo: true
#| lst-label: lst-generalized-poisson-bline-optimizer-r
#| lst-cap: "Direct maximum likelihood for Generalized Poisson regression with underdispersion (R)."
#| message: false
# Negative log-likelihood of the joint Generalized Poisson model
bline_negloglik_r <- function(params, y, X) {
  k <- ncol(X)
  beta <- params[1:k]
  z <- params[k + 1]
  theta <- 2 * plogis(z) - 1      # logistic map to (-1, 1)
  mu <- exp(as.numeric(X %*% beta))
  lam <- (1 - theta) * mu
  t <- lam + theta * y
  if (any(t <= 0)) return(Inf)    # support/truncation guard
  -sum(log(lam) + (y - 1) * log(t) - t - lgamma(y + 1))
}

# One-call fitter: returns coefficients, dispersion, and standard errors
bline_gp_fit_r <- function(y, X) {
  k <- ncol(X)
  start <- c(log(mean(y)), rep(0, k - 1), 0)
  fit <- optim(
    start, bline_negloglik_r, y = y, X = X,
    method = "BFGS", hessian = TRUE, control = list(maxit = 1000)
  )
  se <- sqrt(diag(solve(fit$hessian)))
  beta_hat <- fit$par[1:k]
  se_beta <- se[1:k]
  z_hat <- fit$par[k + 1]
  theta_hat <- 2 * plogis(z_hat) - 1
  se_theta <- 2 * plogis(z_hat) * (1 - plogis(z_hat)) * se[k + 1]
  list(
    beta = beta_hat, se_beta = se_beta,
    theta = theta_hat, se_theta = se_theta, z = z_hat,
    loglik = -fit$value
  )
}

bline_df_simple_r <- length(y_bline_train_r) - (ncol(X_bline_simple_r) + 1)

mu_bline_simple_r <- exp(as.numeric(X_bline_simple_r %*% fit_bline_simple_r$beta))

# Pearson statistic under the GP variance function
bline_pearson_simple_r <- sum(
  (y_bline_train_r - mu_bline_simple_r)^2 /
    (mu_bline_simple_r / (1 - theta_hat_bline_simple_r)^2)
)
c(
  Pearson = round(bline_pearson_simple_r, 2),
  df = bline_df_simple_r,
  p_value = round(pchisq(bline_pearson_simple_r, bline_df_simple_r, lower.tail = FALSE), 3)

# Residual deviance against the saturated GP model
bline_ll_sat_simple_r <- vapply(
  y_bline_train_r,
  function(yi) {
    if (yi == 0) return(0)
    lam_sat <- (
      yi * (1 - theta_hat_bline_simple_r) +
        sqrt(yi^2 * (1 - theta_hat_bline_simple_r)^2 + 4 * theta_hat_bline_simple_r * yi)
    ) / 2
    gp_logpmf_r(yi, lam_sat, theta_hat_bline_simple_r)
  },
  numeric(1)
)

  bline_ll_sat_simple_r -

  p_value = round(pchisq(bline_deviance_simple_r, bline_df_simple_r, lower.tail = FALSE), 3)

# Expected number of zeros under the fitted model

**Briefing — 99 B-Line rush-hour reliability review.**

Across 200 weekday rush-hour windows, the 99 B-Line delivered about 20 bus arrivals per hour at the central stop, and — the review's headline — those arrivals were **significantly more regular than random scheduling would produce**. After modelling the counts with a Generalized Poisson regression, the implied variance of hourly arrivals is roughly `r round(100*(1/(1-theta_hat_bline_test_r)^2), 0)`% of what the standard Poisson benchmark would assign at the same mean (testing-scale ratio `r round(1/(1-theta_hat_bline_test_r)^2, 2)`; $p \approx `r signif(bline_p_theta_test_r, 2)`$ on held-out data). Peak service on the corridor is tight, as the dispatch protocol intends.

**Confidence.** The regularity finding is robust: it appears in both halves of the data, at nearly identical strength, and survives out-of-sample refitting. The secondary findings are weaker. Rainy rush hours show slightly fewer arrivals — about `r round(100*(1-bline_rain_ratio_r["estimate"]), 0)`% fewer per hour — but the association does not reach conventional significance on the held-out refit, so it should be treated as suggestive rather than established. Day-to-day differences were jointly detectable in the model-development data but were too small individually to be decision-relevant, and the extra weekday parameters did not improve predictions on new windows.

**Recommendations and limits.** For reliability KPIs, schedule-adherence dashboards, and passenger-information variance margins, the uncertainty bands calibrated to a Poisson assumption are too wide — roughly a fifth too wide here — so service targets set on those bands would be needlessly conservative. Nothing here licenses causal claims: the rain pattern reflects conditions *concurrent* with rain (traffic, boarding times, seasonal timetables), not the effect of weather alone; and the analysis covers rush-hour behaviour at one corridor — other routes, or off-peak windows where dispatch control is looser, could plausibly be **over**dispersed and would need their own fit.
