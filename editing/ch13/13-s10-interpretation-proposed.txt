## Interpreting the Parameter Estimates {#sec-generalized-poisson-interpretation}

The extended fit of @sec-generalized-poisson-extended-model gives eight regression coefficients and one dispersion parameter. The coefficient interpretation follows Chapter 10's log-link logic exactly: on the log scale a coefficient changes the log expected count additively, so its exponential is a multiplicative **expected-count ratio**. What the Generalized Poisson layer changes is how much uncertainty we attach to those ratios — and, through $\theta$, how we describe the variability a given profile should expect.

### Interpreting the Continuous Regressors {#sec-generalized-poisson-continuous-regressors}

- **Shell width** ($\hat\beta_1 = `r round(cs_ext["width_cm", "Estimate"], 3)`$). Holding the other regressors fixed, a one-centimetre larger carapace is associated with an expected satellite count multiplied by $e^{\hat\beta_1} = `r round(exp(cs_ext["width_cm", "Estimate"]), 3)`$, a Wald 95% interval of (`r round(exp(cs_ext["width_cm", "Estimate"] - 1.96 * cs_ext["width_cm", "Std. Error"]), 2)`, `r round(exp(cs_ext["width_cm", "Estimate"] + 1.96 * cs_ext["width_cm", "Std. Error"]), 2)`) for that ratio. The point estimate is positive — larger crabs attract more satellites — but the interval includes $1$, so the training fit alone does not separate the width effect from zero once the other regressors share the credit.
- **Weight** ($\hat\beta_2 = `r round(cs_ext["weight_kg", "Estimate"], 3)`$). Holding the other regressors fixed, one additional kilogram is associated with an expected count multiplied by $e^{\hat\beta_2} = `r round(exp(cs_ext["weight_kg", "Estimate"]), 2)`$, with interval (`r round(exp(cs_ext["weight_kg", "Estimate"] - 1.96 * cs_ext["weight_kg", "Std. Error"]), 2)`, `r round(exp(cs_ext["weight_kg", "Estimate"] + 1.96 * cs_ext["weight_kg", "Std. Error"]), 2)`). One kilogram is a large step relative to the observed range (`r round(min(training_data$weight_kg), 1)`–`r round(max(training_data$weight_kg), 1)` kg), so it is also instructive to rescale: per 0.1 kg, the ratio is $e^{\hat\beta_2/10} = `r round(exp(cs_ext["weight_kg", "Estimate"] / 10), 3)`$.

::: {.Heads-up}
:::: {.Heads-up-header}
Heads-up on interpreting width and weight together!
::::
:::: {.Heads-up-container}
In the training data the correlation between `width_cm` and `weight_kg` is `r round(cor(training_data$width_cm, training_data$weight_kg), 2)` — the two body-size measures largely carry the same information. "Holding the other fixed" is then a mathematical operation, not a description of a crab one is likely to see: a crab cannot easily gain a kilogram at fixed width. The individual width and weight intervals overlap substantially because the two regressors share their explanatory power; joint interpretation of the *size signal* (both coefficients positive, their linear combination tightly positive) is safer than strong claims about either coefficient on its own.
::::
:::

### Interpreting the Categorical Regressors {#sec-generalized-poisson-categorical-regressors}

For the categorical regressors, every coefficient is a contrast with the reference category (`Light` for colour, `Both good` for spine condition), so an exponentiated coefficient reads as the expected-count ratio *of the named category relative to its reference, other regressors held fixed*.

- **Colour.** Relative to a `Light` female of the same size and spine condition: `Medium light` $e^{\hat\beta_3} = `r round(exp(cs_ext["colorMedium light", "Estimate"]), 2)`$ (95% interval (`r round(exp(cs_ext["colorMedium light", "Estimate"] - 1.96 * cs_ext["colorMedium light", "Std. Error"]), 2)`, `r round(exp(cs_ext["colorMedium light", "Estimate"] + 1.96 * cs_ext["colorMedium light", "Std. Error"]), 2)`)), `Medium dark` $e^{\hat\beta_4} = `r round(exp(cs_ext["colorMedium dark", "Estimate"]), 2)`$ ((`r round(exp(cs_ext["colorMedium dark", "Estimate"] - 1.96 * cs_ext["colorMedium dark", "Std. Error"]), 2)`, `r round(exp(cs_ext["colorMedium dark", "Estimate"] + 1.96 * cs_ext["colorMedium dark", "Std. Error"]), 2)`)), and `Dark` $e^{\hat\beta_5} = `r round(exp(cs_ext["colorDark", "Estimate"]), 2)`$ ((`r round(exp(cs_ext["colorDark", "Estimate"] - 1.96 * cs_ext["colorDark", "Std. Error"]), 2)`, `r round(exp(cs_ext["colorDark", "Estimate"] + 1.96 * cs_ext["colorDark", "Std. Error"]), 2)`)). The estimates decrease monotonically: the point estimate for a `Dark` crab is about `r round(100 * (1 - exp(cs_ext["colorDark", "Estimate"])))`% **fewer** expected satellites than an otherwise identical `Light` crab, and its interval sits almost entirely below one. This direction matches the EDA gradient (@tbl-generalized-poisson-eda-categorical-r).
- **Spine condition.** Relative to `Both good`: `One worn or broken` $e^{\hat\beta_6} = `r round(exp(cs_ext["spineOne worn or broken", "Estimate"]), 2)`$ ((`r round(exp(cs_ext["spineOne worn or broken", "Estimate"] - 1.96 * cs_ext["spineOne worn or broken", "Std. Error"]), 2)`, `r round(exp(cs_ext["spineOne worn or broken", "Estimate"] + 1.96 * cs_ext["spineOne worn or broken", "Std. Error"]), 2)`)) and `Both worn or broken` $e^{\hat\beta_7} = `r round(exp(cs_ext["spineBoth worn or broken", "Estimate"]), 2)`$ ((`r round(exp(cs_ext["spineBoth worn or broken", "Estimate"] - 1.96 * cs_ext["spineBoth worn or broken", "Std. Error"]), 2)`, `r round(exp(cs_ext["spineBoth worn or broken", "Estimate"] + 1.96 * cs_ext["spineBoth worn or broken", "Std. Error"]), 2)`)). Both point estimates are above one, but both intervals comfortably include one; the small `One worn or broken` group (7 training crabs) has a particularly wide interval, as flagged in the EDA.

A results-sentence template for these fits, with placeholders filled from the training model: *"Among female crabs in the training data, and holding the other listed regressors fixed, [category] females had an estimated [ratio]-fold expected number of satellite males relative to [reference] females (95% Wald interval for the ratio: [lo]–[hi])."*

### Interpreting the Dispersion Parameter {#sec-generalized-poisson-theta-interpretation}

The dispersion parameter deserves interpretation of its own, because it is the reason this chapter exists. With $\hat\theta = `r round(theta_hat_ext, 3)`$, any crab profile with fitted expected count $\hat\mu_i$ carries

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(Y_i) = \frac{\hat\mu_i}{(1 - \hat\theta)^2} = `r round(1 / (1 - theta_hat_ext)^2, 2)`\,\hat\mu_i,
\qquad
\widehat{\operatorname{SD}}(Y_i) = \frac{\sqrt{\hat\mu_i}}{1 - \hat\theta} = `r round(1 / (1 - theta_hat_ext), 2)`\,\sqrt{\hat\mu_i}.
$${#eq-generalized-poisson-theta-reading}

A concrete calibration makes the meaning tangible. For a profile with $\hat\mu_i = 3$: the GP model says counts fluctuate with variance `r round(3 / (1 - theta_hat_ext)^2, 1)` (SD $`r round(sqrt(3 / (1 - theta_hat_ext)^2), 1)`$), whereas a Poisson model with the same mean says variance $3$ (SD $`r round(sqrt(3), 2)`$). The GP model also implies $P(Y_i = 0) = \exp(-(1 - \hat\theta)\hat\mu_i) = `r round(exp(-(1 - theta_hat_ext) * 3), 3)`$ — versus `r round(exp(-3), 3)` under the Poisson model at the same mean. The dispersion parameter thus changes not only the widths of confidence intervals but the *shape* of every prediction this model makes: it more than quadruples the implied chance of an empty nest at this mean.

:::{.Tip}
::::{.Tip-header}
Tip: reporting theta alongside the coefficients!
::::
::::{.Tip-container}
A one-line convention that travels well into reports and papers: **"fitted GP dispersion $\hat\theta = `r round(theta_hat_ext, 2)`$ (95% Wald interval `r round(theta_hat_ext - 1.96 * se_theta_ext, 2)` to `r round(theta_hat_ext + 1.96 * se_theta_ext, 2)`, implying a variance `r round(1 / (1 - theta_hat_ext)^2, 1)` times the mean."** Readers then see both the parameter and its consequence on a familiar scale. Reporting $\hat\theta$ alone is opaque; reporting only the ratio hides which side of the Poisson model the data landed on.
::::
:::
