## Introduction {#sec-generalized-poisson-introduction}

This chapter extends [Classical Poisson regression](#sec-classical-poisson) by introducing a more flexible count-regression model. Classical Poisson regression enforces **equidispersion**: the conditional mean and conditional variance of the count response must be equal. In practice, this assumption is frequently violated. When the variance *exceeds* the mean, the data are **overdispersed**; when the variance falls *below* the mean, they are **underdispersed**. Under overdispersion, Classical Poisson regression underestimates standard errors, inflating the risk of Type I error. Under underdispersion, it overestimates standard errors, making genuine associations harder to detect — and the model has no mechanism to represent underdispersion at all.

The Generalized Poisson (GP) distribution, introduced by @consul1973, relaxes this restriction by adding a single dispersion parameter $\theta$. When $\theta = 0$, the GP distribution reduces exactly to the Poisson distribution; when $\theta > 0$ it accommodates overdispersion; and when $\theta < 0$ it accommodates underdispersion. [Negative Binomial regression](#sec-negative-binomial) also addresses overdispersion, but only the GP model covers underdispersion as well, and because the Classical Poisson model is nested inside the GP family, the two models can be compared directly with a likelihood-ratio test.

Throughout this chapter, we revisit the horseshoe crab satellite counts from @sec-classical-poisson. In that chapter, the Classical Poisson goodness-of-fit checks signalled a problem with the crab data: the residual deviance was substantially larger than its degrees of freedom, and the mean–variance check showed clear overdispersion. Generalized Poisson regression is precisely the remedy this situation calls for, so the crab data give us a running case study with a concrete starting point — one fitted model whose adequacy failed, and one more flexible distribution that can absorb the extra variability. The opposite direction of failure, underdispersion, is harder to find in classic teaching datasets; we return to it in the practice exercises, where a second case study applies the complete workflow to hourly arrivals of the 99 B-Line bus at the UBC Bus Loop, counts that vary *less* than a Poisson process would allow.

Some research questions you might explore using Generalized Poisson regression:

- How is the **expected number of satellite male crabs** around a female horseshoe crab nest associated with her shell width and colour, once the overdispersion in the counts is accounted for?
- How are **hourly bus arrival counts** associated with the day of the week and weather conditions when an actively managed schedule makes the counts more regular than a Poisson process would predict (developed in the practice exercises)?
- How is the **expected number of workplace injury claims per employee** associated with industry sector and firm size, when claim counts are far more variable than their mean?

### Generalized Poisson Regression Assumptions {#sec-generalized-poisson-assumptions}

The assumptions of Generalized Poisson regression parallel those of Classical Poisson regression, with one key modification:

- **Independence**: each count response $Y_i$ is independent of all the others, with its own positive expected count $\mu_i > 0$.
- **Log-linearity**: the log of the expected count is modelled as a linear combination of the regressors, $\log(\mu_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i,1} + \cdots + \beta_k x_{i,k}$.
- **Dispersion flexibility**: unlike the Classical Poisson model, the conditional variance need not equal the conditional mean. The GP model allows $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i) \neq \mathbb{E}(Y_i)$ through the relationship $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i) = \mu_i / (1 - \theta)^2$, where the dispersion parameter $\theta \in (-1, 1)$ controls the direction and magnitude of the departure from equidispersion.

### A Graphical Look {#sec-generalized-poisson-graphical-look}

Before developing the model formally, @fig-gp-graphical-look gives a visual preview of what the dispersion parameter $\theta$ does. Inspect the spread of each panel relative to the dashed Classical Poisson reference: the middle panel spreads probability mass further from the mean, while the right panel concentrates it more tightly.

::: {#fig-gp-graphical-look}
![](img/gp_dispersion_figure.png)

The effect of the dispersion parameter $\theta$ on the shape of the Generalized Poisson distribution (all panels: mean = 5). Left: Classical Poisson ($\theta = 0$, variance-to-mean ratio 1.00). Middle: overdispersed GP ($\theta = 0.4$, ratio 2.78), wider spread and heavier tails than the Poisson reference. Right: underdispersed GP ($\theta = -0.4$, ratio 0.51), counts cluster tightly around the mean with far less spread than the Poisson reference. The dashed line in the middle and right panels shows the Classical Poisson shape for comparison.

### Documented Use Cases {#sec-generalized-poisson-use-cases}

Generalized Poisson regression and its close relatives are established tools for count data whose variability does not match the Poisson model. @tbl-generalized-poisson-use-cases summarizes several documented uses, from the distribution's methodological foundations to applied analyses. The side by side appearance of overdispersed and underdispersed applications is the point: a single model family covers both directions.
