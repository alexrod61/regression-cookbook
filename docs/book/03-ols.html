3  Ordinary Least-squares Regression

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  • Determine when Ordinary Least-squares (OLS) regression is an appropriate modelling choice for a continuous response variable.
  • Define a multiple linear regression model by identifying the response variable and regressors.
  • Explain how OLS estimates regression coefficients by minimizing the sum of squared residuals.
  • Be familiar with the matrix form of linear regression and connect it to the algebra behind OLS estimation.
  • Fit an OLS model using Python or R to estiamte the coefficients in the model.
  • Interpret regression coefficients to assess associations between regressors and the response variable while holding other regressors fixed.
  • Interpret binary, categorical, and ordinal regressors, including the use of dummy variables and the numeric treatment of ordinal variables.
  • Use a fitted OLS model to generate predictions for the response variable.
  • Check key OLS assumptions, including linearity, independence, homoscedasticity, and normality of residuals, and describe possible strategies for addressing assumption violations.
  • Evaluate model performance using \(R^2\) and error metrics such as mean absolute error (MAE), mean squared error (MSE), and root mean squared error (RMSE).

When to Use and Not Use Ordinary Least-squares Regression

Ordinary Least-squares regression is a classical linear model that is appropriately used when the following conditions are reasonably satisfied:

  • The outcome variable is continuous. Examples include temperature, pressure differences, electrical voltages, monetary profits or losses, and net changes in blood pressure. The outcome should not be a count or binary variable.

  • The conditional mean of the outcome can be reasonably represented as a linear combination of the regressors.

  • The observations, and therefore the corresponding errors, are approximately independent. Whether this assumption is reasonable should be considered in the context of the study design.

  • The variance of the errors is approximately constant across observations; this condition is known as homoscedasticity.

  • For classical finite-sample statistical inference, we additionally assume that the errors are Normally distributed.

Ordinary Least-squares regression may not be appropriate, or may require modification, in the following scenarios:

  • The outcome variable is discrete, such as a count or binary response. For discrete outcomes, you can refer to the regression models found in the “discrete cuisine” of this book. For strictly positive continuous outcomes whose distribution is strongly right-skewed and whose variance changes with the mean, Gamma regression may be a more appropriate alternative (see Chapter 4).

  • The conditional mean has a strong non-linear relationship with the regressors that is not adequately represented by the linear model. Possible approaches include adding appropriate transformations or nonlinear terms, or using a generalized additive model (GAM), as introduced by Hastie and Tibshirani (1986). A useful resource on GAMs can be found in Clark (2022).

  • The observations exhibit substantial dependence or correlation, such as repeated measurements or clustered observations. In such settings, a Linear Mixed-effects (LME) regression model may be more appropriate. A helpful illustrative resource on LME can be found in Wu (2009).

  • The variance of the errors is not constant; this phenomenon is known as heteroscedasticity. Depending on the setting, possible approaches include Weighted Least-squares (WLS) regression or methods that adjust the estimated standard errors for heteroscedasticity. Insights on WLS are provided by Dodge (2008).

  • Outliers, high-leverage observations, or influential observations may substantially affect the fitted OLS model. These observations should first be investigated rather than automatically removed. In some settings, robust regression methods may also be considered (Rousseeuw and Leroy 1987).

3.1 Introduction

In many data science problems, we are interested in understanding how a continuous outcome changes with respect to several other regressors (also known as independent or explanatory variables). For example, in a dataset about student finances, we may want to understand why some students have more money left over at the end of the month than others. Is it because they have a job? Is it because they receive a monthly allowance? Does housing situation matter? Do spending habits help explain differences in financial balance?

These questions naturally lead us to regression modelling. Regression allows us to study the relationship between a response variable and one or more regressors. A useful feature of regression modelling is that it allows us to examine relationships while accounting for several variables at the same time. For example, students with jobs may also have higher monthly earnings, and students who receive financial support may differ from those who do not in other ways. Rather than looking at each variable separately a regression model allows us to estimate the association between each regressor and the response while holding the other regressors fixed.

In this chapter, we use Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression, one of the most widely used methods for modelling a continuous response variable. We will use OLS to examine how several student characteristics are associated with financial balance, while introducing the main ideas behind fitting, interpreting, and evaluating a regression model.

3.2 What is Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)?

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) is a fundamental method in regression analysis for estimating the relationship between a continuous response variable and one or more regressors. In simple linear regression, we can think of OLS as drawing the best-fitting straight line through a scatterplot of data points. Imagine plotting students’ net savings on a graph, where each point represents a student’s financial outcome. OLS finds the line that best represents the relationship between the regressor and the response by minimizing the sum of the squared differences between the observed and predicted values.

OLS is widely used because it is:

  • Simple: The basic idea is straightforward to understand and implement.
  • Clear: The estimated coefficients provide interpretable measures of the association between each regressor and the response variable.
  • Versatile: OLS is widely used across many fields, including economics, social sciences, health sciences, and data science.

In this chapter, we will break down how OLS works in plain language, explore its underlying assumptions, and discuss its practical applications and limitations. This will give you a solid foundation in regression analysis, paving the way for more advanced techniques later on in this book.

3.3 The “Best Line”

When using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) with a single regressor, our goal is to find the straight line that best represents the relationship between the response variable \(Y\) and the regressor \(x\). But what does “best” mean?

Imagine a scatterplot in which each point represents an observed pair of values \((x_i, Y_i)\). Now consider drawing two different lines through the data. Each line produces a predicted response value for every observed value of \(x\).

  • Line A (Blue): A line that follows the general trend of the data well.
  • Line B (Red): A line that does not capture the trend as well.

Visually, Line A appears to provide a better representation of the relationship between \(x\) and \(Y\). However, OLS does not choose the best-fitting line based only on visual inspection. Instead, it uses a numerical criterion based on the differences between the observed response values and the values predicted by the line.

3.3.1 Understanding Residuals

For each observation, the residual is the difference between the observed response value \(Y_i\) and the response value predicted by the fitted regression line, denoted by \(\hat{Y}_i\). For the \(i\)-th observation, the residual is

\[ e_i = Y_i - \hat{Y}_i. \tag{3.1}\]

Here, \(Y_i\) represents the observed response value for the \(i\)-th observation, while \(\hat{Y}_i\) represents the corresponding predicted response value from the fitted model. Graphically, the residual is the signed vertical difference between the observed data point and the fitted regression line at the same value of \(x\).

A positive residual means that the observed response lies above the fitted line, whereas a negative residual means that the observed response lies below the fitted line. When the fitted line represents the data well, we generally expect the residuals to be relatively small.

OLS quantifies how well a line fits the observed data using the sum of squared residuals, commonly referred to as the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE). It is calculated by:

  1. Computing the residual \(e_i = Y_i - \hat{Y}_i\) for each observation.
  2. Squaring each residual so that positive and negative residuals do not cancel each other out.
  3. Summing the squared residuals across all \(n\) observations.

Mathematically, the SSE is

\[ SSE = \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(Y_i - \hat{Y}_i\right)^2. \tag{3.2}\]

A smaller SSE indicates that the predicted values are, overall, closer to the observed response values. Using the criterion defined in Equation 3.2, Ordinary Least Squares chooses the regression line whose coefficient estimates produce the smallest possible SSE.

3.3.2 Quantifying the Fit with SSE

We can now compare Line A and Line B quantitatively using the SSE defined in Equation 3.2. For each line, we calculate the residual for every observation, square these residuals, and then sum them across all observations. The line with the smaller SSE provides the better least-squares fit to the observed data.

3.3.3 Why Squared Errors?

Residuals can be either positive or negative. If we simply added the residuals together, positive and negative values could cancel each other out, making a poorly fitting model appear better than it actually is. Squaring each residual avoids this problem because every squared residual is non-negative.

Squaring also gives greater weight to larger residuals. For example, a residual of \(10\) contributes \(10^2 = 100\) to the SSE, whereas a residual of \(2\) contributes only \(2^2 = 4\). As a result, observations with large residuals have a greater influence on the least-squares criterion.

This is why OLS uses the sum of squared residuals rather than the simple sum of residuals. The fitted regression coefficients are chosen to make this quantity, defined in Equation 3.2, as small as possible.

3.3.4 The Mathematical Formulation of the OLS Model

Now that we understand how OLS chooses the best-fitting line by minimizing the sum of squared residuals, we can describe the model more formally.

Suppose we observe \(n\) independent observations. For the \(i\)-th observation, let \(Y_i\) denote the continuous response variable and let \(x_i\) denote the value of a single regressor, where \(i = 1, 2, \ldots, n\). Here, \(i = 1, 2, \ldots, n\) indexes the observations in the dataset, where \(n\) is the total number of observations.

We define the conditional mean of the response as

\[ \mu_i = \mathbb{E}(Y_i \mid x_i), \tag{3.3}\]

where \(\mu_i\) represents the expected value of the response for an observation with regressor value \(x_i\).

In a simple linear regression model, this expected response is modeled as a linear function of the regressor:

\[ \mu_i = \eta_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i, \tag{3.4}\]

where \(\eta_i\) is called the linear predictor. In OLS regression, the conditional mean \(\mu_i\) is directly equal to the linear predictor \(\eta_i\). This corresponds to using an identity link:

\[ g(\mu_i) = \mu_i = \eta_i. \tag{3.5}\]

For now, we assume that the conditional mean \(\mu_i\) is equal to the linear predictor \(\eta_i\). This relationship is called the identity link, and we will discuss more of these link functions with more detail in later chapters.

The observed response \(Y_i\) will generally not fall exactly on its expected value \(\mu_i\). We account for this unexplained variation using an error term \(\epsilon_i\):

\[ Y_i = \mu_i + \epsilon_i. \tag{3.6}\]

Substituting the linear predictor from Equation 3.4 gives the familiar simple linear regression model:

\[ Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \epsilon_i. \tag{3.7}\]

where:

  • \(Y_i\) is the observed response value for the \(i\)-th observation.
  • \(\mu_i = \mathbb{E}(Y_i \mid x_i)\) is the expected response value given \(x_i\).
  • \(x_i\) is the observed value of the regressor for the \(i\)-th observation.
  • \(\eta_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i\) is the linear predictor.
  • \(\beta_0\) is the intercept, representing the expected value of \(Y_i\) when \(x_i = 0\).
  • \(\beta_1\) is the slope coefficient, representing the expected change in \(Y_i\) associated with a one-unit increase in \(x_i\).
  • \(\epsilon_i\) is the random error term, representing variation in \(Y_i\) that is not explained by the linear relationship with \(x_i\).

OLS coefficient estimates can be computed without imposing distributional assumptions on the error terms. However, assumptions about the error terms are important for establishing statistical properties of the OLS estimator and for performing statistical inference, such as constructing confidence intervals or conducting hypothesis tests (which we will see later in this chapter).

For the classical linear regression model used for statistical inference, we assume that the error terms are independent, have mean zero, and have constant variance:

\[ \mathbb{E}(\epsilon_i \mid x_i) = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\epsilon_i \mid x_i) = \sigma^2. \tag{3.8}\]

For hypothesis tests and confidence intervals under the classical Normal linear model, we additionally assume

\[ \epsilon_i \mid x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2). \tag{3.9}\]

This notation means that, for a given value of the regressor \(x_i\), the error term \(\epsilon_i\) is assumed to follow a Normal distribution with mean \(0\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). In other words, the errors are centered around zero, and \(\sigma^2\) represents the amount of variability in the response that is not explained by the regression model.

Equivalently, the conditional distribution of the response can be written as

\[ Y_i \mid x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i,\sigma^2), \qquad \mu_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i. \tag{3.10}\]

This formulation highlights the three main components of the model: 1) the response distribution, 2) the linear predictor, and 3) the identity link connecting the expected response to the regressors. Later in this chapter, we will extend this formulation from a single regressor to multiple regressors.

3.5 Study Design

Now that we have introduced the case study and defined the main statistical question, we need to clarify the purpose of our analysis. In this case, our primary goal is to understand how different student characteristics are associated with Net_Money rather than simply producing accurate predictions.

To make this distinction more precise, we consider two common goals of regression analysis: inference and prediction.

3.5.1 Inferential vs. Predictive Analysis

Following the data science workflow, the purpose of the analysis determines how we fit, evaluate, and interpret a regression model.

  • Inferential analysis focuses on understanding and quantifying the relationship between the regressors and the response variable. In our case, we are interested in estimating how characteristics such as employment status, monthly earnings, and spending behavior are associated with Net_Money, while accounting for the other regressors in the model. Statistical inference also allows us to quantify uncertainty in these estimated relationships using tools such as standard errors, confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests.

  • Predictive analysis focuses on accurately predicting the response for new observations. For example, we might ask how accurately we can predict a student’s Net_Money based on their financial and demographic characteristics. In this setting, the emphasis is primarily on predictive performance rather than on interpreting individual regression coefficients.

OLS can be used for either purpose. In this case study, our primary focus is inferential, because we want to understand and quantify the relationships between student characteristics and Net_Money. For example, we are interested in questions such as:

  • Is having a job associated with differences in a student’s expected Net_Money?

  • How is monthly earnings associated with expected Net_Money?

  • How are spending behaviors, such as going out and spending money more frequently, associated with expected Net_Money?

Later in the chapter, we will also briefly examine the predictive performance of the fitted OLS model to illustrate how the same model can be used to predict Net_Money for new observations.

3.6 Data Collection and Wrangling

With the statistical questions clearly defined, the next step is to consider how the data were collected and prepared for analysis. Although the dataset is already available, understanding how the data could have been collected helps us assess its context, quality, and potential limitations.

3.6.1 Data Collection

Because this is a toy dataset, there is no actual data collection process to describe. However, in a real study addressing similar questions, data on students’ financial behaviors could be collected through several sources:

  • Surveys: Students could be asked about their employment status, earnings, financial support, and spending habits through structured questionnaires. Although surveys can capture information that may not be available from administrative records, they can be affected by recall bias or social desirability bias.

  • Administrative Data: Universities, employers, or other institutions may maintain records related to student employment, financial aid, or earnings. Such records can provide more objective information, although access may be limited because of privacy and confidentiality requirements.

  • Financial Tracking Apps: Digital financial management tools can provide detailed information on income and spending patterns over time. However, using these data may introduce selection bias because students who use financial tracking apps may differ systematically from those who do not.

Regardless of the data collection method, potential issues such as missing data, measurement or reporting errors, and selection bias should be considered. Addressing these issues is an important part of preparing data for analysis. A detailed treatment of data collection methods and sampling design is beyond the scope of this book.

1. Checking for Missing Values

An important step in data preparation is checking for missing values. Missing data can occur for many reasons, such as unanswered survey questions, unrecorded measurements, or data-entry errors. If missing values are present, we need to decide how they should be handled before fitting the regression model.

Common approaches include:

  • Removing incomplete observations: Excluding observations with missing values when doing so is appropriate for the analysis.

  • Imputing missing values: Replacing missing values using an appropriate imputation method so that the observations can remain in the analysis.

Heads-up on missing values!

The appropriate treatment of missing data depends on why the values are missing and on the goals of the analysis. Different approaches can lead to different conclusions. A detailed treatment of missing-data mechanisms and imputation methods is beyond the scope of this book, but analysts should always report how missing values were handled and consider how these choices may affect the results.

3. Examining Potential Outliers

Continuous variables such as Monthly_Earnings and Net_Money may contain observations that are unusually large or small compared with the rest of the data. Because unusual observations can have a substantial influence on an OLS regression model, they should be examined carefully rather than automatically removed.

One common exploratory approach is the Interquartile Range (IQR) rule, which flags observations that fall below \(Q_1 - 1.5 \times IQR\) or above \(Q_3 + 1.5 \times IQR\) as potential outliers. Importantly, being flagged by this rule does not necessarily mean that an observation is incorrect or should be excluded from the analysis.

  • R Code
  • Python Code
# Function to identify potential outliers using the IQR rule
identify_outliers <- function(x) {
  Q1 <- quantile(x, 0.25, na.rm = TRUE)
  Q3 <- quantile(x, 0.75, na.rm = TRUE)
  IQR_value <- Q3 - Q1

  x < (Q1 - 1.5 * IQR_value) | x > (Q3 + 1.5 * IQR_value)
}

3.8 Data Modelling

After completing the exploratory data analysis, the next step is to develop a statistical model that describes the relationship between the response variable and one or more regressors. In this chapter, we focus on ordinary least squares (OLS) regression, one of the most widely used modelling approaches in statistics, data science, economics, and machine learning.

Regression models may be used for inference, prediction, or both. In inferential settings, the focus is on estimating and interpreting the relationships between regressors and the response variable. In predictive settings, the primary goal is to accurately predict the response for new or unseen observations.

Building a regression model involves more than simply applying functions in R or Python. It requires deciding which variables to include, determining how they should be represented in the model, fitting the model, evaluating its assumptions, and refining it when necessary. Insights obtained during exploratory data analysis can help guide these decisions.

Depending on the problem, regressors may also require additional modelling choices, such as creating indicator variables for categorical regressors, applying transformations, or including polynomial or interaction terms.

In the following sections, we introduce the OLS model for this case study, describe how its regression coefficients are estimated, and show how to fit and interpret the model in both R and Python.

3.8.1 Choosing a Suitable Regression Model

The choice of regression model depends on the characteristics of the response variable, the relationships observed during EDA, and the goals of the analysis. Different regression approaches are suitable for different types of relationships and modelling objectives. Some common approaches include:

3.8.2 Defining the Regression Model

After selecting multiple linear regression fitted using OLS, we now specify the model mathematically. Suppose we observe \(n\) independent students, indexed by \(i = 1, \ldots, n\). Let \(Y_i\) denote the value of Net_Money for the \(i\)-th student, and let \(\mathbf{x}_i\) represent the collection of regressors observed for that student.

For each observation, we define the conditional mean of the response as

\[ \mu_i = \mathbb{E}(Y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i). \tag{3.11}\]

In a multiple linear regression model, this conditional mean is represented as a linear combination of the regressors:

\[ \mu_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i,1} + \beta_2 x_{i,2} + \cdots + \beta_k x_{i,k}. \tag{3.12}\]

Equivalently, using the linear predictor \(\eta_i\) definition,

\[ \eta_i = \beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_j x_{i,j}, \]

and for ordinary linear regression we use the identity link,

\[ \mu_i = \eta_i. \tag{3.13}\]

The observed response will generally differ from its conditional mean. We represent this unexplained variation using an error term \(\epsilon_i\):

\[ Y_i = \mu_i + \epsilon_i. \tag{3.14}\]

Combining these expressions gives the general multiple linear regression model

\[ Y_i = \beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_j x_{i,j} + \epsilon_i. \tag{3.15}\]

For the classical linear model, we assume that the errors have conditional mean zero and constant variance,

\[ \mathbb{E}(\epsilon_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = 0, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\epsilon_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \sigma^2. \]

When we additionally assume Normally distributed errors, the model can be written equivalently as

\[ Y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i,\sigma^2), \tag{3.16}\]

where

\[ \mu_i = \beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_j x_{i,j}. \]

This Normal formulation will become particularly useful in the Estimation section, where we will show how the likelihood of the model is connected to the ordinary least-squares criterion.

For this case study, the regressors are defined as follows:

  • \(x_{i,1}\) represents Has_Job, coded as

\[ x_{i,1} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if the student has a job},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

  • \(x_{i,2}\) represents Year_of_Study. This ordinal variable is retained in numerical form, so a one-unit increase corresponds to moving from one year of study to the next.

  • \(x_{i,3}\) represents Financially_Dependent, coded as

\[ x_{i,3} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if the student is financially dependent},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

  • \(x_{i,4}\) represents Monthly_Allowance, measured in Canadian dollars.

  • \(x_{i,5}\) represents Cooks_at_Home, coded as

\[ x_{i,5} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if the student cooks at home regularly},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

  • \(x_{i,6}\) represents Goes_Out_Spends_Money, an ordinal variable measured on a scale from \(1\) (rarely) to \(5\) (very often). As with Year_of_Study, this variable is retained in numerical form in the model.

  • \(x_{i,7}\) represents Drinks_Alcohol, coded as

\[ x_{i,7} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if the student drinks alcohol},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

  • \(x_{i,8}\) represents Monthly_Earnings, measured in Canadian dollars.

The categorical regressors Living_Situation and Housing_Type require indicator variables because each contains more than two categories.

For Living_Situation, the reference category is Family. We define

\[ x_{i,9} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if the student lives in shared housing},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases} \]

and

\[ x_{i,10} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if the student lives alone},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

For Housing_Type, the reference category is Rent. We define

\[ x_{i,11} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if the student lives in owned housing},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases} \]

and

\[ x_{i,12} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if the student lives in a dormitory},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

The subscript \(i\) indexes individual students in the dataset, while the second subscript identifies the regressor. Together, the regressors represent the financial, demographic, and behavioral characteristics included in our model.

Substituting these regressors into the multiple linear regression model gives

\[ \underbrace{Y_i}_{\text{Response}} = \underbrace{ \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i,1} + \beta_2 x_{i,2} + \beta_3 x_{i,3} + \cdots + \beta_{12} x_{i,12} }_{\text{Systematic component}} + \underbrace{\epsilon_i}_{\text{Random component}}. \tag{3.17}\]

Here,

  • \(\beta_0\) is the intercept.

  • \(\beta_1, \beta_2, \ldots, \beta_{12}\) are the regression coefficients describing the association between each regressor and the expected response.

  • \(\epsilon_i\) is the random error term representing variation in Net_Money that is not explained by the regressors included in the model.

Each regression coefficient has an interpretation that depends on the type of regressor. For a numerical regressor, the coefficient represents the expected change in Net_Money associated with a one-unit increase in that regressor, while holding all other regressors fixed. For an indicator variable, the coefficient represents the expected difference in Net_Money between the corresponding category and its reference category, again holding the other regressors fixed.

For example:

  • \(\beta_4\) represents the expected change in Net_Money associated with a one-dollar increase in Monthly_Allowance, holding the other regressors fixed.

  • \(\beta_7\) represents the expected difference in Net_Money between students who drink alcohol and students who do not, holding the other regressors fixed.

  • \(\beta_{10}\) represents the expected difference in Net_Money between students who live alone and students who live with family, holding the other regressors fixed.

The intercept \(\beta_0\) represents the expected value of Net_Money when all numerical regressors are equal to zero and all categorical regressors are at their reference categories. Depending on the values that are meaningful for the regressors, the intercept may not always have a useful practical interpretation.

Although interaction terms or nonlinear terms could be added to extend this model, we do not include them in the current case study. We begin with the additive multiple linear regression model defined above and later assess its adequacy using model diagnostics.

3.9 Estimation

With the regression model specified, we now turn to estimation. The unknown parameters \(\beta_0, \beta_1, \ldots, \beta_k\) describe the relationship between the regressors and the conditional mean of the response. Because these parameters are unknown, we use the observed data to estimate them.

Let

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\hat{\beta}_0,\hat{\beta}_1,\ldots,\hat{\beta}_k)^\top \]

denote the estimated regression coefficients. For the \(i\)-th observation, the fitted response value is

\[ \widehat{Y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{k}\hat{\beta}_j x_{i,j}. \tag{3.18}\]

The difference between the observed response \(Y_i\) and its fitted value \(\widehat{Y}_i\) is called the residual:

\[ e_i = Y_i-\widehat{Y}_i. \tag{3.19}\]

Ordinary least squares estimates the regression coefficients by choosing the values that make these residuals collectively as small as possible. More specifically, OLS minimizes the sum of squared errors (SSE),

\[ SSE(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ Y_i - \left( \beta_0+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_j x_{i,j} \right) \right]^2. \tag{3.20}\]

The OLS estimator can therefore be written as

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \underset{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{\operatorname{argmin}} \; SSE(\boldsymbol{\beta}). \tag{3.21}\]

This formalizes the idea of the “best-fitting” model introduced earlier in the chapter. In simple linear regression, OLS chooses the line that minimizes the sum of squared residuals. With multiple regressors, the same principle applies, although geometrically the fitted model represents a hyperplane rather than a single line.

So far, we have described OLS as an optimization problem based on squared residuals. However, there is also an important probabilistic interpretation. Under the Normal linear regression model introduced earlier, the same coefficient estimates can be obtained using maximum likelihood estimation. We examine this connection next.

3.9.1 The Likelihood Perspective

Ordinary least squares can also be viewed from a probabilistic perspective. Recall that under the classical Normal linear regression model, we assume

\[ Y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i,\sigma^2), \]

where

\[ \mu_i = \beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_j x_{i,j}. \]

Here, \(\mu_i\) is the conditional mean of the response for the \(i\)-th observation, and \(\sigma^2\) represents the conditional variance of the response around that mean. Equivalently, this corresponds to assuming

\[ \epsilon_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2). \]

For an observed response value \(y_i\), the Normal probability density is therefore

\[ f(y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left[ -\frac{ \left( y_i-\beta_0-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_j x_{i,j} \right)^2 }{ 2\sigma^2 } \right], \tag{3.22}\]

where

\[ \boldsymbol{\beta} = (\beta_0,\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_k)^\top \]

is the vector of regression coefficients.

The density in Equation 3.22 describes how plausible a particular observed response \(y_i\) is for given values of the model parameters. The likelihood function reverses this perspective: we treat the observed data as fixed and view the likelihood as a function of the unknown parameters \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) and \(\sigma^2\).

Because the observations are assumed to be independent, the joint likelihood for all \(n\) observations is the product of their individual Normal densities:

\[ L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f(y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2). \]

Substituting the Normal density gives

\[ L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left[ -\frac{ \left( y_i-\beta_0-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_j x_{i,j} \right)^2 }{ 2\sigma^2 } \right]. \tag{3.23}\]

Collecting the terms, we can write the likelihood more compactly as

\[ L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2} \exp\left[ -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( y_i-\beta_0-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_jx_{i,j} \right)^2 \right]. \tag{3.24}\]

Notice that the summation appearing inside the exponential function is exactly the sum of squared errors:

\[ SSE(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( y_i-\beta_0-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_jx_{i,j} \right)^2. \]

Therefore, the likelihood can also be written as

\[ L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2} \exp\left[ -\frac{SSE(\boldsymbol{\beta})}{2\sigma^2} \right]. \tag{3.25}\]

Rather than maximizing the likelihood directly, it is usually easier to work with its natural logarithm. Because the logarithm is a strictly increasing function, the values of the parameters that maximize the likelihood also maximize the log-likelihood:

\[ \ell(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2) = \log L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2). \]

Taking the logarithm of Equation 3.25 gives

\[ \ell(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi) -\frac{n}{2}\log(\sigma^2) -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}SSE(\boldsymbol{\beta}). \tag{3.26}\]

Now consider estimation of the regression coefficients \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\). For a fixed value of \(\sigma^2\), the first two terms in Equation 3.26 do not depend on \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\). Since \(\sigma^2 > 0\), maximizing the log-likelihood with respect to \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is therefore equivalent to minimizing

\[ SSE(\boldsymbol{\beta}). \]

In other words,

\[ \underset{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{\operatorname{argmax}} \; L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^2) = \underset{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{\operatorname{argmin}} \; SSE(\boldsymbol{\beta}). \tag{3.27}\]

This establishes an important connection: under the Normal linear regression model, the maximum likelihood estimates of the regression coefficients are the same as the ordinary least-squares estimates.

Therefore,

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{MLE}} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{OLS}}. \]

The Normal likelihood also allows us to estimate the error variance \(\sigma^2\). After substituting the estimated regression coefficients, maximizing the likelihood with respect to \(\sigma^2\) gives

\[ \hat{\sigma}^2_{\mathrm{MLE}} = \frac{SSE(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{n}. \tag{3.28}\]

However, the maximum likelihood estimator in Equation 3.28 is biased downward as an estimator of the population error variance. In classical linear regression, we therefore commonly use the degrees-of-freedom-adjusted estimator

\[ \hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{SSE(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})} {n-k-1}, \tag{3.29}\]

where \(k\) is the number of regressors and \(k+1\) parameters are estimated when the intercept is included.

The likelihood perspective therefore leads to the same coefficient estimates as the least-squares criterion introduced earlier. The remaining question is how we obtain these coefficient estimates explicitly. The matrix formulation of OLS provides a convenient way to derive the closed-form solution.

The Matrix Form of Linear Regression

The multiple linear regression model can be written compactly using matrix notation as

\[ \mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}, \tag{3.30}\]

where:

  • \(\mathbf{Y}\) is an \(n \times 1\) vector containing the observed response values.
  • \(\mathbf{X}\) is an \(n \times (k+1)\) design matrix, where \(k\) is the number of regressors. The additional column corresponds to the intercept.
  • \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is a \((k+1) \times 1\) vector containing the unknown regression coefficients, including the intercept.
  • \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) is an \(n \times 1\) vector containing the random error terms.

For \(n\) observations and \(k\) regressors, the design matrix has the form

\[ \mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_{1,1} & x_{1,2} & \cdots & x_{1,k} \\ 1 & x_{2,1} & x_{2,2} & \cdots & x_{2,k} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_{n,1} & x_{n,2} & \cdots & x_{n,k} \end{bmatrix}. \tag{3.31}\]

Each row of \(\mathbf{X}\) corresponds to one observation, while each column after the first corresponds to a regressor. The element \(x_{i,j}\) therefore represents the value of the \(j\)-th regressor for the \(i\)-th observation.

The first column contains only ones and represents the intercept term. Including this column allows the model to estimate an intercept rather than forcing the fitted regression line, or more generally the fitted hyperplane, to pass through the origin.

For example, suppose we have data from \(n=100\) individuals and want to model salary using two regressors: years of experience and years of education. The response vector \(\mathbf{Y}\) contains the 100 observed salary values and is therefore a \(100 \times 1\) vector. Since the model contains two regressors plus an intercept, the design matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) has 100 rows and 3 columns.

A small portion of the design matrix might look like

\[ \mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 5 & 16 \\ 1 & 2 & 14 \\ 1 & 8 & 18 \\ 1 & 10 & 20 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \end{bmatrix}, \]

where:

  • the first column represents the intercept,
  • the second column contains years of experience,
  • the third column contains years of education,
  • and each row represents one individual.

The corresponding coefficient vector is

\[ \boldsymbol{\beta} = \begin{bmatrix} \beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \\ \beta_2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Recall that OLS estimates the coefficient vector by minimizing the sum of squared errors. In scalar notation,

\[ SSE(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( Y_i-\beta_0-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_jx_{i,j} \right)^2. \]

Using matrix notation, the same objective can be written more compactly as

\[ SSE(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = (\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})^\top (\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}). \tag{3.32}\]

The superscript \({}^\top\) denotes the transpose operation. Since \(\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is an \(n \times 1\) vector, multiplying its transpose by itself produces a scalar equal to the sum of its squared elements.

To find the value of \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) that minimizes the SSE, we differentiate the objective function with respect to \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) and set the derivative equal to zero.

Starting with

\[ SSE(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = (\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})^\top (\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}), \]

we first expand the expression:

\[ SSE(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \left( \mathbf{Y}^\top - (\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})^\top \right) (\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}). \]

Using the transpose rule

\[ (\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})^\top = \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top, \]

we obtain

\[ SSE(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = (\mathbf{Y}^\top-\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top) (\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}). \]

Multiplying the terms gives

\[ SSE(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \mathbf{Y}^\top\mathbf{Y} - \mathbf{Y}^\top\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} - \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{Y} + \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}. \]

The two middle terms are scalars and are equal:

\[ \mathbf{Y}^\top\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} = \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{Y}. \]

Therefore,

\[ SSE(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \mathbf{Y}^\top\mathbf{Y} - 2\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{Y} + \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}. \]

Differentiating with respect to \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) gives

\[ \frac{\partial SSE(\boldsymbol{\beta})} {\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}} = -2\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{Y} + 2\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}. \tag{3.33}\]

Setting the gradient equal to zero gives

\[ -2\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{Y} + 2\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{0}. \]

After simplifying, we obtain the normal equations:

\[ \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{Y}. \tag{3.34}\]

If the columns of \(\mathbf{X}\) are linearly independent, then \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) is invertible. Solving the normal equations gives the OLS estimator

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{Y} }. \tag{3.35}\]

The second derivative matrix, or Hessian, of the SSE is

\[ \frac{\partial^2 SSE(\boldsymbol{\beta})} {\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top} = 2\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}. \]

When the columns of \(\mathbf{X}\) are linearly independent, \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) is positive definite. Therefore, the solution in Equation 3.35 corresponds to the unique minimum of the SSE.

This expression is the ordinary least-squares estimator. It shows explicitly how the observed response vector \(\mathbf{Y}\) and the design matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) determine the estimated regression coefficients.

Once the coefficients have been estimated, the fitted values can be written as

\[ \widehat{\mathbf{Y}} = \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \]

and the vector of residuals is

\[ \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{Y}-\widehat{\mathbf{Y}} = \mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}. \]

Notice the distinction between the error vector \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) in the statistical model and the residual vector \(\mathbf{e}\) obtained after fitting the model. The errors are unobserved random quantities, whereas the residuals can be calculated from the observed data and the fitted model.

The matrix

\[ \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{X} (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \tag{3.36}\]

is called the hat matrix because it maps the observed response vector \(\mathbf{Y}\) to the fitted response vector \(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}\):

\[ \hat{\mathbf{Y}} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{Y}. \]

The diagonal elements of \(\mathbf{H}\), denoted \(h_{ii}\), are called leverage values. They measure how unusual an observation’s regressor values are relative to those of the other observations. We will return to leverage when discussing influential observations in the Goodness of Fit section.

In practice, statistical software such as lm() in R and ols() in Python computes the coefficient estimates using numerically stable matrix algorithms rather than explicitly forming the inverse \((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\).

Nevertheless, the closed-form expression above is useful for understanding the mathematical foundation of OLS and why, unlike many other regression models in this book, its regression coefficients can be obtained without iterative numerical optimization.

A note on ordinal regressors

Some regressors are ordered categories. In this example, Year_of_Study and Goes_Out_Spends_Money are ordinal variables because their values have a natural ordering. For instance, year 4 comes after year 3, and a spending-frequency score of 5 represents more frequent spending than a score of 4.

In this model, we treat these ordinal variables as numeric regressors. This is a modelling choice. It means that we assume a one-unit increase has a constant linear association with the response variable, holding the other regressors fixed. For example, the model assumes that the expected change in Net_Money from year 1 to year 2 is the same as the expected change from year 3 to year 4.

This approach keeps the model simpler and easier to interpret. Instead of estimating a separate coefficient for each level of an ordinal variable, we estimate one coefficient that summarizes the average linear change across the ordered scale.

For example, suppose the estimated coefficient for Year_of_Study is \(-100\). Then, holding all other regressors fixed, moving up by one academic year is associated with an estimated decrease of 100 CAD in expected Net_Money, on average.

Similarly, suppose the estimated coefficient for Goes_Out_Spends_Money is \(-50\). Since this variable is treated as numeric, a one-unit increase on the spending-frequency scale is associated with an estimated decrease of 50 CAD in expected Net_Money, on average, holding the other regressors fixed.

This numeric treatment is appropriate when a linear trend across the ordered categories is a reasonable approximation. If we do not want to assume a constant one-unit association, we could instead represent the ordinal variable using indicator variables and compare each level with a reference category.

3.10 Goodness of Fit

After estimating the regression coefficients, the next step is to assess how well the model fits the training data and whether the assumptions of Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression are reasonable. This evaluation helps us determine whether the fitted model is appropriate for interpretation and statistical inference.

At this stage, we focus on the model fitted to the training data. Generalization to unseen observations will be assessed separately using predictive performance on the test data. We examine both the overall fit of the model and a series of diagnostic checks related to the OLS assumptions. Serious violations of these assumptions can affect coefficient estimates, standard errors, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, or predictions.

3.10.1 Checking Model Assumptions

The OLS model relies on several assumptions about the relationship between the regressors, the response variable, and the random error terms. The main assumptions we examine in this chapter are:

  • Linearity

  • Independence of errors

  • Homoscedasticity, or constant error variance

  • Normality of errors

These assumptions play different roles in OLS regression. Some are important for the statistical properties of the coefficient estimates, while others are particularly important for standard errors, confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests which we introduce later in this chapter. We assess whether these assumptions are reasonable using diagnostic plots and statistical tests (where appropriate).

Linearity

A core assumption of OLS regression is that the conditional mean of the response variable is appropriately represented as a linear combination of the regressors. If important nonlinear relationships are not represented in the model, the residuals may display systematic patterns. This indicates that the linear model does not adequately capture the relationship between the regressors and the response variable.

A common diagnostic for assessing the linearity of the fitted model is a Residuals vs. Fitted plot. In a well-specified linear model, the residuals should be scattered around zero without a clear systematic pattern. Curved or other structured patterns may suggest that nonlinear terms, transformations, or other changes to the model should be considered.

To assess this assumption, we plot the standardized residuals against the fitted values:

Independence of Errors

Another assumption of the classical linear regression model is that the error terms are independent across observations. In other words, the unexplained component associated with one observation should not systematically depend on the unexplained component associated with another observation.

This assumption is particularly important for data with a natural ordering, such as time-series or longitudinal data, where errors from nearby observations may be correlated. Such dependence is commonly referred to as autocorrelation. When errors are correlated, the usual OLS standard errors may be incorrect, which can affect confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.

For the current dataset, observations correspond to different students rather than repeated measurements over time. Therefore, the independence assumption is primarily supported by the data collection and study design. Nevertheless, we use the Durbin-Watson statistic as an additional diagnostic for first-order autocorrelation in the residuals.

The Durbin-Watson statistic is defined as

\[ DW = \frac{ \sum_{i=2}^{n} (e_i - e_{i-1})^2 }{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i^2 }, \tag{3.37}\]

where \(e_i\) is the residual for observation \(i\). The Durbin-Watson statistic is not itself the autocorrelation coefficient. Instead, for first-order autocorrelation, it is approximately related to the residual autocorrelation \(\rho\) by

\[ DW \approx 2(1-\rho). \]

Therefore, under the null hypothesis of no first-order autocorrelation,

\[ H_0:\rho = 0, \]

we expect the Durbin-Watson statistic to be close to 2. Values near 2 suggest little evidence of first-order autocorrelation, values substantially below 2 are associated with positive autocorrelation, and values substantially above 2 are associated with negative autocorrelation.

In R, the default dwtest() tests for positive autocorrelation:

\[ H_0: \rho = 0, \]

\[ H_1: \rho > 0, \]

where \(\rho\) represents the first-order autocorrelation parameter.

Distribution of the Durbin-Watson Statistic

Under the null hypothesis of no autocorrelation, the Durbin-Watson statistic does not follow a simple standard distribution such as a \(t\)-distribution or a \(\chi^2\) distribution. Its distribution depends on the design matrix \(X\) used in the regression model.

More formally, under the null hypothesis, the Durbin-Watson statistic can be written as a ratio of weighted sums of squared standard Normal random variables:

\[ d \sim \frac{ \sum_{i=1}^{n-k-1} \nu_i \xi_i^2 }{ \sum_{i=1}^{n-k-1} \xi_i^2 }, \]

where \(\xi_1, \ldots, \xi_{n-k-1}\) are independent standard Normal random variables, and \(\nu_1, \ldots, \nu_{n-k-1}\) are eigenvalues that depend on the regression design matrix \(X\).

The main takeaway is that the Durbin-Watson test has a special null distribution that depends on the fitted regression model. In practice, software computes or approximates the relevant p-value for us, so we usually focus on interpreting the statistic and its p-value.

A note on autocorrelation

If the test suggests autocorrelation, a possible solution is to use time-series regression models such as Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) or introduce lagged predictors to account for dependencies in the data. These models are beyond the scope of this textbook, but readers interested in time-series models and ARIMA models can consult (BrockwellDavis?).

Note that because this dataset is not a time series, the independence assumption is primarily justified by the data collection design. The Durbin-Watson statistic is included here as an additional diagnostic but it is most relevant when observations have a natural ordering.

Homoscedasticity (Constant Variance of Errors)

OLS regression assumes that the conditional variance of the error terms is constant across observations. This assumption is called homoscedasticity and can be written as

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\epsilon_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \sigma^2. \]

If the error variance changes systematically with the regressors or fitted values, the model shows heteroscedasticity. Under heteroscedasticity, the OLS coefficient estimates can still be unbiased under the other classical assumptions. However, the usual estimated standard errors may be incorrect. As a result, confidence intervals and hypothesis tests based on those standard errors may be unreliable.

We can assess constant variance visually using the Residuals vs. Fitted plot introduced earlier. If the spread of the residuals remains reasonably similar across the range of fitted values, this is consistent with the constant-variance assumption. A funnel-shaped pattern, where the spread systematically increases or decreases, may indicate heteroscedasticity.

We can also assess this assumption formally using the Breusch-Pagan test. The test is based on the idea that, under homoscedasticity, the squared residuals should not be systematically related to the regressors. If the magnitude of the squared residuals can be explained by the regressors, this provides evidence that the conditional error variance changes across observations.

The Breusch-Pagan test can be written as

\[ H_0: \text{The error variance is constant.} \]

\[ H_1: \text{The error variance is not constant.} \]

One way to construct the Breusch-Pagan test is to obtain the residuals from the fitted OLS model, square them, and regress the squared residuals on the regressors. The test statistic is commonly written as

\[ BP = nR^2_{\text{aux}}, \]

where \(n\) is the sample size and \(R^2_{\text{aux}}\) is the coefficient of determination from this auxiliary regression. Under the null hypothesis of constant variance, the test statistic is asymptotically distributed as a chi-square random variable, with degrees of freedom corresponding to the number of regressors tested in the auxiliary regression.

A small p-value provides evidence against the null hypothesis and suggests that heteroscedasticity may be present.

Normality of Errors

For statistical inference in OLS regression, we additionally assume that the error terms are Normally distributed conditional on the regressors:

\[ \epsilon_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2). \]

Because the error terms \(\epsilon_i\) are unobserved, we assess this assumption using the residuals from the fitted model. A Q-Q (Quantile-Quantile) plot compares the quantiles of the standardized residuals with the theoretical quantiles of a Normal distribution. If the error distribution is approximately Normal, the points should lie reasonably close to the reference line.

3.10.2 Evaluating Model Fit

R-Squared and Adjusted R-Squared

Beyond checking the model assumptions, it is also useful to assess how much of the observed variation in the response variable is explained by the fitted regression model. One of the most commonly used goodness-of-fit measures for OLS regression is R-squared (\(R^2\)), also known as the coefficient of determination.

The \(R^2\) statistic is defined as

\[ R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}, \tag{3.38}\]

where

\[ \text{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 \tag{3.39}\]

is the sum of squared errors, and

\[ \text{SST} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2 \tag{3.40}\]

is the total sum of squares. Here, \(y_i\) is the observed response value, \(\hat{y}_i\) is the fitted response value, and \(\bar{y}\) is the sample mean of the response variable.

The rationale behind \(R^2\) in Equation 3.38 is to compare the fitted regression model with a simple baseline model that always predicts the sample mean \(\bar{y}\). If the regression model substantially reduces the unexplained variation relative to this baseline, then \(\text{SSE}\) will be much smaller than \(\text{SST}\) and \(R^2\) will be closer to 1. If the fitted model provides little improvement over predicting the sample mean, then \(\text{SSE}\) will be close to \(\text{SST}\) and \(R^2\) will be closer to 0.

A larger \(R^2\) indicates that the fitted model explains a greater proportion of the observed variation in the response variable. However, the usefulness of a particular \(R^2\) value depends on the context and purpose of the analysis.

Because \(R^2\) never decreases when additional regressors are added to the model, we often also report adjusted R-squared. Adjusted R-squared accounts for model complexity and is defined as:

\[ R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - \left( \frac{n - 1}{n - p - 1} \right) (1 - R^2), \tag{3.41}\]

where \(n\) is the sample size and \(p\) is the number of estimated slope coefficients, excluding the intercept. For categorical regressors represented by multiple indicator variables, each estimated indicator coefficient contributes to \(p\).

Adjusted R-squared penalizes the inclusion of additional regressors that provide little improvement in model fit and is therefore useful when comparing models with different numbers of estimated coefficients.

3.10.3 Additional Model Diagnostics

Multicollinearity

In multiple regression, we also need to consider whether some regressors are strongly linearly related to the other regressors in the model. This issue is called multicollinearity.

Multicollinearity does not necessarily imply poor overall model fit, but it can make individual coefficient estimates unstable and difficult to interpret. In particular, it can inflate the variances and standard errors of estimated coefficients, making it harder to distinguish the association of one regressor from the associations of the others.

For a regressor represented by a single coefficient, a common diagnostic is the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). The VIF measures how much the variance of an estimated coefficient is inflated because of linear relationships between that regressor and the remaining regressors. For the \(j\)th regressor,

\[ \text{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}, \tag{3.42}\]

where \(R_j^2\) is obtained by regressing the \(j\)th regressor on the remaining regressors in the model. If a regressor can be well explained by the other regressors, then \(R_j^2\) will be large, making the VIF large. A VIF close to 1 indicates little evidence of multicollinearity involving that regressor.

As rough rules of thumb, VIF values greater than 5 may deserve further investigation, while values greater than 10 are often considered evidence of substantial multicollinearity. These thresholds should not be treated as strict decision rules.

Unlike the Breusch-Pagan or Durbin-Watson procedures, VIF is a diagnostic measure rather than a formal hypothesis test. We therefore examine the magnitude of the VIF values together with the structure and interpretation of the regressors.

Generalized VIF

When a regression model includes a categorical regressor with more than two levels, that regressor is represented by multiple indicator variables and therefore contributes more than one coefficient to the model. In this situation, the ordinary Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for a single coefficient is not sufficient to describe multicollinearity for the categorical regressor as a whole.

The car::vif() function therefore reports the generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF) for model terms with more than one degree of freedom. To make GVIF values more comparable across terms with different numbers of degrees of freedom, we can examine

\[ \text{GVIF}^{1/(2 \times \text{Df})}, \]

where \(\text{Df}\) is the number of degrees of freedom associated with the model term.

Values of this adjusted measure close to 1 indicate little evidence of multicollinearity. For the current model, the adjusted GVIF values for Living_Situation and Housing_Type are both very close to 1, indicating little evidence that these categorical regressors are strongly related to the remaining regressors.

For model terms with one degree of freedom, the reported GVIF is equivalent to the usual VIF. Therefore, for variables such as Has_Job and Monthly_Earnings, we can interpret the GVIF values using the usual VIF scale. More details on generalized VIF are available in (GVIF?).

Outliers, Leverage, and Influential Observations

Another important part of regression diagnostics is identifying observations that may behave differently from the majority of the data or have an unusually large influence on the fitted model. Three related concepts are useful here: outliers, high-leverage observations, and influential observations.

An outlier is an observation whose observed response value is unusually far from the value predicted by the model. Such observations tend to have relatively large residuals. For observation \(i\), the residual is

\[ e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i, \]

where \(y_i\) is the observed response value and \(\hat{y}_i\) is the fitted response value.

A high-leverage observation has an unusual combination of regressor values relative to the other observations in the dataset. High leverage does not necessarily mean that an observation is poorly fit by the model, but such observations have greater potential to affect the fitted regression coefficients.

An influential observation is one whose inclusion or removal has a noticeable effect on the fitted regression model. Influence therefore depends on both how unusual an observation is in terms of its regressors and how poorly it is fit by the model. An observation may be an outlier without being highly influential, and a high-leverage observation is not necessarily influential if it is well aligned with the fitted regression relationship.

We begin by examining the residuals across observations. Plotting residuals against the observation index can help identify observations with unusually large positive or negative residuals. This is primarily a diagnostic procedure rather than a formal hypothesis test, so we inspect whether any observations stand out clearly from the rest of the data.

Another important diagnostic tool is Cook’s Distance, which measures how strongly each observation influences the fitted regression model. Cook’s Distance combines information about both the residual size and the leverage of an observation. An observation with a large residual, high leverage, or both may therefore have a relatively large Cook’s Distance.

For observation \(i\), Cook’s Distance can be written as

\[ D_i = \frac{ \sum_{j=1}^{n} (\hat{y}_j - \hat{y}_{j(i)})^2 }{ p \, \widehat{\sigma}^2 }, \tag{3.43}\]

where \(\hat{y}_j\) is the fitted value for observation \(j\) using the full dataset, \(\hat{y}_{j(i)}\) is the fitted value for observation \(j\) after removing observation \(i\), \(p\) is the total number of estimated regression parameters including the intercept, and \(\widehat{\sigma}^2\) is the estimated error variance.

The rationale behind Cook’s Distance is to ask how much the fitted model would change if a particular observation were removed. Larger values indicate observations with greater influence on the fitted model.

There is no universal cutoff that determines whether an observation is influential. One commonly used screening guideline is

\[ D_i > \frac{4}{n}, \]

where \(n\) is the number of observations used to fit the model. Observations exceeding this guideline may deserve further investigation, particularly when their Cook’s Distance values also stand out relative to the rest of the data. This threshold should be treated as a diagnostic guideline rather than an automatic rule for removing observations.

As with the residual and leverage diagnostics, Cook’s Distance is not a formal hypothesis test. We inspect the values and investigate observations that appear potentially influential.

Robust Regression and Influential Observations

If one or more observations appear to have a strong influence on the fitted OLS model, it is important to investigate them carefully before deciding what to do. Influential observations may result from data-entry errors, but they may also be valid observations representing unusual yet meaningful cases. Therefore, observations should not be removed simply because they have large residuals, high leverage, or relatively large Cook’s Distance values.

A useful first step is to perform a sensitivity analysis by fitting the model with and without the potentially influential observations and examining whether the main conclusions change substantially. If the estimated coefficients or inferential conclusions are highly sensitive to a small number of observations, this should be reported and investigated further.

Another possible approach is to compare the OLS model with a robust regression model. Robust regression methods are designed to reduce the influence of observations that depart substantially from the fitted relationship. For example, instead of minimizing squared residuals as in OLS, some robust methods use alternative loss functions that give less weight to observations with very large residuals.

However, not all robust regression methods handle high-leverage observations in the same way. Some methods primarily protect against observations with unusually large residuals, while more specialized robust methods are designed to remain stable in the presence of both outliers and high-leverage observations. Robust regression should therefore be viewed as a possible sensitivity analysis rather than an automatic replacement for OLS whenever an influential observation is detected.

A detailed treatment of robust regression is beyond the scope of this book but readers interested in robust regression and regression diagnostics can consult Rousseeuw and Leroy (1987).

3.11.1 Inferential Analysis

To address our inferential question, we fit the pre-specified OLS model to the test data, which contains 200 observations. We then examine the estimated regression coefficients, standard errors, confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests to determine which student characteristics show statistical evidence of association with Net_Money, while holding the other regressors fixed.

For each regression coefficient, we test whether there is evidence that the corresponding regressor is associated with the conditional mean of Net_Money, after accounting for the other regressors in the model. For the \(j\)th regression coefficient, we test

\[ H_0: \beta_j = 0, \]

against

\[ H_1: \beta_j \neq 0. \]

Under \(H_0\), the \(j\)th regressor does not contribute a linear association with the conditional mean of Net_Money after holding the other regressors fixed. Under \(H_1\), there is evidence of an association.

The test statistic for the \(j\)th regression coefficient is

\[ t_j = \frac{\hat{\beta}_j}{SE(\hat{\beta}_j)}, \tag{3.44}\]

where \(\hat{\beta}_j\) is the estimated regression coefficient and \(SE(\hat{\beta}_j)\) is its estimated standard error.

Under the null hypothesis \(H_0: \beta_j = 0\), this statistic follows a \(t\)-distribution with \(n-k-1\) degrees of freedom, where \(n\) is the sample size and \(k\) is the number of regressors in the model excluding the intercept.

The p-value measures the strength of the evidence against the null hypothesis. A small p-value indicates that the observed test statistic would be unlikely if \(H_0\) were true. In this chapter, we use a significance level of

\[ \alpha = 0.05. \]

If the p-value is less than 0.05, we reject \(H_0\) and conclude that there is statistical evidence that the corresponding regressor is associated with the conditional mean of Net_Money, holding the other regressors fixed. If the p-value is greater than or equal to 0.05, we fail to reject \(H_0\).

Performance Metrics

Model predictive performance is assessed on the test data using four common regression metrics: Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Mean Squared Error (MSE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and R-squared (\(R^2\)). These metrics compare the observed Net_Money values in the test data with the predictions generated by the model fitted on the training data.

For observation \(i\) in the test data, let \(y_i\) denote the observed value of Net_Money and let \(\hat{y}_i\) denote the prediction generated by the model fitted on the training data. The prediction error for observation \(i\) is

\[ e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i. \]

A positive prediction error indicates that the model underpredicts Net_Money, while a negative prediction error indicates that the model overpredicts it.

Let \(n_{\text{test}}\) denote the number of observations in the test data:

  • The Mean Absolute Error (MAE) measures the average absolute prediction error:

\[ \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n_{\text{test}}} \sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{test}}} |y_i - \hat{y}_i|. \]

MAE is measured in the same units as the response variable. In this case, it represents the average absolute difference, in CAD, between the observed and predicted values of Net_Money. Smaller values indicate better predictive performance.

  • The Mean Squared Error (MSE) measures the average squared prediction error:

\[ \text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n_{\text{test}}} \sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{test}}} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2. \]

Because prediction errors are squared, MSE gives greater weight to larger errors. Smaller values indicate better predictive performance. However, MSE is expressed in squared CAD, which makes it less directly interpretable than MAE or RMSE.

  • The Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) is the square root of MSE:

\[ \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{n_{\text{test}}} \sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{test}}} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 }. \]

RMSE is expressed in the same units as Net_Money, making it easier to interpret than MSE. Like MSE, RMSE gives greater weight to larger prediction errors. Smaller values indicate better predictive performance.

  • The test-set R-squared (\(R^2\)) compares the model’s prediction error with a baseline that predicts the mean response in the test data:

\[ R^2_{\text{test}} = 1 - \frac{ \sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{test}}}(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2 }{ \sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{test}}}(y_i-\bar{y}_{\text{test}})^2 }, \]

where \(\bar{y}_{\text{test}}\) is the mean of the observed Net_Money values in the test data. Values closer to 1 indicate better predictive performance. A value of 0 indicates that the model performs no better than predicting the test-set mean, while a negative value indicates that the model performs worse than this baseline.

Together, these metrics provide complementary measures of predictive performance. MAE summarizes the typical absolute prediction error, MSE and RMSE place greater emphasis on larger errors, and test-set \(R^2\) evaluates predictive performance relative to a simple mean-based baseline.

Confidence Interval versus Prediction Interval

A 95% confidence interval for the expected mean response describes uncertainty about the mean Net_Money for students with a particular set of characteristics. In other words, it provides an interval estimate for the average financial balance among students who share the same regressor values.

A 95% prediction interval describes uncertainty about the Net_Money value for one new individual student with those same characteristics. A prediction interval is wider because it accounts for both uncertainty in estimating the conditional mean and the additional variability of individual observations around that mean.

For a new student profile with regressor vector \(\mathbf{x}_0\), a 95% confidence interval for the expected mean response has the form

\[ \hat{y}_0 \pm t_{0.975,\,n-k-1} \,SE(\hat{y}_0), \]

where \(\hat{y}_0\) is the estimated conditional mean response for the new profile and \(SE(\hat{y}_0)\) is the standard error associated with estimating that mean response.

A 95% prediction interval for a new individual response has the form

\[ \hat{y}_0 \pm t_{0.975,\,n-k-1} \sqrt{ \widehat{\sigma}^2 + SE(\hat{y}_0)^2 }. \]

The additional \(\widehat{\sigma}^2\) term represents the unexplained variability of individual observations around the regression model. This additional source of uncertainty is why prediction intervals are generally wider than confidence intervals for the expected mean response.

Answer 3.1

Correct answer: A.

Rationale:

OLS estimation is equivalent to maximizing a Normal likelihood when

\[ Y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \sigma^2), \]

The Poisson and Bernoulli assumptions are used for count and binary responses, respectively, and their likelihoods lead to different estimation procedures. Therefore, a Normal conditional response with constant variance is the distributional assumption that connects OLS and MLE.

OLS minimizes the sum of squared residuals because squaring prevents positive and negative residuals from cancelling each other out. If we minimized the ordinary sum of residuals, a model with large positive and negative errors could appear to fit well simply because the errors cancel. Squaring also gives a smooth objective function, which leads to convenient mathematical and computational properties. Using absolute errors creates a non-smooth objective function and leads to a different regression method that is generally harder to optimize than ordinary least squares.

Question 3.16

Short Answer

Answer 3.16

Click here to reveal the answer!

Answer:

A training-data \(R^2\) of 0.89 indicates that the fitted model accounts for about 89% of the observed variation in the response variable within the training data. However, a higher \(R^2\) is not always better because \(R^2\) cannot decrease when additional regressors are added, even if those regressors provide little useful information.

Question 3.19

Short Answer

Suppose the dataset does not include important financial variables such as student debt or scholarships. Answer 3.19

Click here to reveal the answer!

Answer:

A confounding variable is a variable that is related to both the response variable and one or more regressors in the model. For example, scholarships may be related to Net_Money, but they may also be related to whether a student is financially dependent or how much the student needs to work. If scholarships are omitted from the model, part of their association with Net_Money may be incorrectly attributed to included regressors such as Monthly_Earnings or Financially_Dependent. Apartment rent in this simulated dataset is associated with a combination of apartment size, location, building characteristics, and furnishing. After accounting for the other regressors, larger apartments and furnished apartments tend to have higher monthly rents, while apartments farther from downtown and apartments in older buildings tend to have lower rents. Neighbourhood also remains associated with rent after accounting for the other apartment characteristics; for example, apartments in the Central neighbourhood have a higher estimated mean monthly rent than otherwise comparable apartments in the Suburban reference neighbourhood.

The model also performs well from a predictive perspective. When the model fitted on the training data is applied to the protected test data, the MAE is approximately 144 CAD, the RMSE is approximately 184 CAD, and the test-set \(R^2\) is approximately 0.875. For a 900-square-foot, two-bedroom, furnished apartment located 5 km from downtown in the West neighbourhood and in a 10-year-old building, the model predicts a monthly rent of approximately 2,245 CAD. The 95% prediction interval for one apartment with these characteristics ranges from approximately 1,867 CAD to 2,622 CAD.

Because these data are observational, the estimated relationships should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. The analysis describes how apartment characteristics and monthly rent vary together in this simulated dataset; it does not establish that changing one characteristic would necessarily cause rent to change by the corresponding regression coefficient.
