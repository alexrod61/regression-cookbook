13  Generalized Poisson Regression

When to Use and Not Use Generalized Poisson Regression

Generalized Poisson regression is a count-regression model for modelling non-negative integer count outcomes when the variability of the counts departs from what the Classical Poisson model allows. It is most appropriate when the response records how many times an event occurs for each observational unit and the counts are visibly more or less variable than their mean. Examples include the number of satellite males around a horseshoe crab nest, the number of workplace injury claims per employee, or the number of actively managed bus arrivals per hour at a transit stop.

Use Generalized Poisson regression when the following conditions are reasonable for the data and the modelling inquiry:

  • The response variable is a non-negative integer count, as illustrated in Figure 13.1.
  • The scientific or data-science inquiry focuses on how the expected count changes with a set of regressors.
  • The observations can be treated as statistically independent, or at least independent enough for the modelling purpose.
  • The expected count is positive and can be connected to the regressors through the log link.
  • The counts display overdispersion (variance exceeding the mean) or underdispersion (variance falling below the mean), making the equal mean–variance restriction of Classical Poisson regression untenable.

Generalized Poisson regression should not be the default choice in the following situations:

  • The response is not a count. Binary outcomes are usually better handled with Binary Logistic regression, proportions require their own modelling choices such as Beta regression, and continuous responses belong to other chapters of the cookbook (such as Ordinary Least-squares or Gamma regressions).
  • The counts are consistent with equidispersion, meaning their conditional variance is approximately equal to their conditional mean. In that case, the simpler Classical Poisson regression is preferable, since the additional dispersion parameter is unnecessary.
  • The overdispersion is driven by an excess of zero counts that a single count distribution cannot explain. In that case, Zero-Inflated Poisson regression may be more appropriate.
  • The observations exhibit strong dependence or clustering — for instance, repeated measurements on the same subjects or spatially correlated counts. In such cases, a generalized linear mixed model may be more appropriate because it can extend the generalized linear model framework with random effects for grouped or dependent observations; see, for example, Bolker et al. (2009) for a practical introduction to generalized linear mixed models in ecological and evolutionary applications.

In this chapter, we use Generalized Poisson regression as the cookbook’s flexible count-regression model for data that are overdispersed or underdispersed. For counts that are only overdispersed, Negative Binomial regression is another standard alternative; only the Generalized Poisson model handles both dispersion directions within a single distribution that nests Classical Poisson as a special case.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  • Explain why Classical Poisson regression can fail when the equidispersion assumption is violated, in either direction.
  • Determine when Generalized Poisson regression is an appropriate modelling choice relative to Classical Poisson, Negative Binomial, and Zero-Inflated Poisson alternatives.
  • Frame inferential and predictive inquiries for a count-regression problem with overdispersed or underdispersed data using the data science workflow.
  • Specify a Generalized Poisson regression model through its random component, its systematic component, and its log link function.
  • Write and interpret the Generalized Poisson probability mass function and its mean–variance relationship, including the role of the dispersion parameter \(\theta\).
  • Explain how maximum likelihood estimation jointly estimates the regression coefficients and the dispersion parameter, and how the assumed variance structure drives model-based standard errors.
  • Assess model adequacy with observed-versus-fitted checks, a dispersion test, a zero-count check, residual deviance, and residual plots, and formally test whether the dispersion parameter departs from zero.
  • Interpret regression coefficients as multiplicative changes in the expected count, holding other regressors fixed, and interpret the estimated dispersion parameter through the implied variance-to-mean ratio.
  • Evaluate predictive performance using held-out prediction metrics and a baseline comparison.
  • Communicate Generalized Poisson regression results responsibly for both inferential and predictive inquiries.

13.1 Introduction

This chapter extends Classical Poisson regression by introducing a more flexible count-regression model. Classical Poisson regression enforces equidispersion: the conditional mean and conditional variance of the count response must be equal. In practice, this assumption is frequently violated. When the variance exceeds the mean, the data are overdispersed; when the variance falls below the mean, they are underdispersed. Under overdispersion, Classical Poisson regression underestimates standard errors, inflating the risk of Type I error. Under underdispersion, it overestimates standard errors, making genuine associations harder to detect — and the model has no mechanism to represent underdispersion at all.

The Generalized Poisson (GP) distribution, introduced by Consul and Jain (1973), relaxes this restriction by adding a single dispersion parameter \(\theta\). When \(\theta = 0\), the GP distribution reduces exactly to the Poisson distribution; when \(\theta > 0\) it accommodates overdispersion; and when \(\theta < 0\) it accommodates underdispersion. Negative Binomial regression also addresses overdispersion, but only the GP model covers underdispersion as well, and because the Classical Poisson model is nested inside the GP family, the two models can be compared directly with a likelihood-ratio test.

Throughout this chapter, we revisit the horseshoe crab satellite counts from Chapter 10. In that chapter, the Classical Poisson goodness-of-fit checks signalled a problem with the crab data: the residual deviance was substantially larger than its degrees of freedom, and the mean–variance check showed clear overdispersion. Generalized Poisson regression is precisely the remedy this situation calls for, so the crab data give us a running case study with a concrete starting point — one fitted model whose adequacy failed, and one more flexible distribution that can absorb the extra variability. The opposite direction of failure, underdispersion, is harder to find in classic teaching datasets; we return to it in the practice exercises, where a second case study applies the complete workflow to hourly arrivals of the 99 B-Line bus at the UBC Bus Loop, counts that vary less than a Poisson process would allow.

Figure 13.1 situates Generalized Poisson regression within the regression techniques covered so far in this book: it is the count-regression model intended for count outcomes that are overdispersed or underdispersed.

Some research questions you might explore using Generalized Poisson regression:

  • How is the expected number of satellite male crabs around a female horseshoe crab nest associated with her shell width and colour, once the overdispersion in the counts is accounted for?
  • How are hourly bus arrival counts associated with the day of the week and weather conditions when an actively managed schedule makes the counts more regular than a Poisson process would predict (developed in the practice exercises)?
  • How is the expected number of workplace injury claims per employee associated with industry sector and firm size, when claim counts are far more variable than their mean?

13.1.1 Generalized Poisson Regression Assumptions

The assumptions of Generalized Poisson regression parallel those of Classical Poisson regression, with one key modification:

  • Independence: each count response \(Y_i\) is independent of all the others, with its own positive expected count \(\mu_i > 0\).
  • Log-linearity: the log of the expected count is modelled as a linear combination of the regressors, \(\log(\mu_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i,1} + \cdots + \beta_k x_{i,k}\).
  • Dispersion flexibility: unlike the Classical Poisson model, the conditional variance need not equal the conditional mean. The GP model allows \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_i) \neq \mathbb{E}(Y_i)\) through the relationship \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_i) = \mu_i / (1 - \theta)^2\), where the dispersion parameter \(\theta \in (-1, 1)\) controls the direction and magnitude of the departure from equidispersion.

13.2 Case Study: Horseshoe Crab Satellites

The running case study in this chapter revisits the horseshoe crab satellites dataset introduced in Chapter 10. Each observational unit is one female horseshoe crab nest, and the response is the number of male satellite crabs residing around the nest. As before, the candidate regressors are observable characteristics of the female crab: her carapace width and weight, her colour category, and her spine condition.

Reusing the same dataset is deliberate. In Chapter 10, the extended Classical Poisson model for these data failed important goodness-of-fit checks: the residual deviance was substantially larger than its degrees of freedom, the Pearson dispersion estimate was well above one, and the observed zero counts exceeded what the fitted model expected. In other words, the crab counts are overdispersed, and the Chapter 10 conclusion pointed explicitly to more flexible count models — including the Generalized Poisson model of this chapter. Returning to the same data lets us ask a sharper question than “how does GP regression work?” It lets us ask: does the extra dispersion parameter actually repair the adequacy failures of the Classical Poisson fit, and what changes in the inferential and predictive conclusions when it does?

Because we already know the dataset well from Chapter 10, the case-study-specific introduction can be brief. The data remain secondary, observational data: no experiment was designed, no treatments were assigned, and no causal claims will be made. Associations between female crab characteristics and the expected number of satellite males will be described as associations, not effects.

13.3 Study Design: Framing the Inferential and Predictive Inquiries

Before fitting a Generalized Poisson regression model, we clarify what the model is supposed to help us learn. This is the study design stage of the data science workflow introduced in Section 1.4.1. At this stage, we are not yet choosing software functions or interpreting coefficients. Instead, we are defining the modelling purpose, the observational unit, the response variable, and the role of the regressors.

For the horseshoe crab case study, the study-design elements are summarized in Table 13.2. This table fixes the basic modelling ingredients before we decide how the Generalized Poisson regression model will be used.

Note that a single dataset can support different kinds of modelling inquiries. Therefore, we will use the same count response to illustrate two related but distinct modelling purposes: an inferential inquiry and a predictive inquiry, as shown in Table 13.3. This distinction is critical since the modelling workflow is not judged in exactly the same way for both purposes.

The above inferential and predictive inquiries are connected but not interchangeable. Keeping them separate helps us decide which results belong in the inferential interpretation and which results belong in the prediction-accuracy discussion.

Heads-up on inference versus prediction!

For the inferential inquiry, the main object of interest is the relationship between the expected count and the regressors. We will pay attention to coefficient estimates, the estimated dispersion parameter, standard errors, confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests, together with the assumptions needed for the interpretation to be reasonable. Here, the model’s variance assumption matters twice: it describes the data and it drives the model-based standard errors.

On the other hand, for the predictive inquiry, the main object of interest is how well the model predicts expected counts for observations not used to fit the model. We will therefore use a training/testing split, compute predicted expected counts on the testing set, compare prediction errors against a baseline model, and summarize prediction accuracy with metrics such as mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and likelihood-based predictive scores.

Both inquiries require model checking. If the Generalized Poisson model fits the data poorly, then both the inferential interpretation and the predictive results become less useful.

13.3.1 Inferential Inquiry

Specifically, the inferential inquiry asks — exactly as it did in Chapter 10:

How is the expected number of satellite males associated with female crab characteristics, holding other regressors fixed?

The question is identical to Chapter 10 on purpose: we want to compare the two model classes on the same scientific footing. The question is about association and interpretation, not causation. The data are observational, and the case study does not establish a causal design. The inferential goal is to describe how the expected count of satellite males changes across observed female crab characteristics within a Generalized Poisson regression framework.

For this inquiry, the results stage will focus on coefficient estimates, the estimated dispersion parameter, uncertainty, and statistical interpretation. A central question is whether the conclusions from Chapter 10 — which relied on Poisson-based standard errors despite failed dispersion checks — survive once the variance assumption is relaxed.

13.3.2 Predictive Inquiry

Now, the predictive inquiry asks:

How well can the available regressors be used to predict the expected number of satellite males for a female crab?

This question is about prediction accuracy. The target of prediction in Generalized Poisson regression is the expected count, not necessarily the exact observed count. For a given female crab, the observed number of satellite males may differ from the model-predicted expected count because count outcomes are variable even when the model is well specified — and the Generalized Poisson model gives that variability an explicit, estimable size through its dispersion parameter.

For this inquiry, the results stage will focus on out-of-sample prediction. We will fit the model using a training set and evaluate prediction accuracy on a testing set. The testing-set predictions will be compared against a simple baseline that predicts the training-set mean count for every testing observation, and against the Classical Poisson fit using the same regressors. These comparisons are necessary because an accuracy metric is easier to interpret when we can ask whether the regression model does better than simple benchmarks.

Tip on the 50/50 split and more careful alternatives!

This dataset contains only 173 observations. A standard predictive modelling workflow often uses a larger training set, such as an 80/20 training/testing split. However, in this chapter we need to support two goals at the same time: an inferential inquiry and a predictive inquiry.

Using a 50/50 split is a compromise. It gives the training set enough observations for EDA, model fitting, and goodness-of-fit checking, while also leaving a testing set large enough to support both the final predictive accuracy assessment and the final inferential refit. This choice is not perfect. A larger training set would usually help estimation and diagnostics, while a larger testing set gives a more informative final assessment. With small datasets, we often have to choose between these inconveniences.

The 50/50 split used here is also a simple random split, which means it does not explicitly protect against imbalance across important variables. For example, if one female crab colour category or spine condition is rare, a simple random split could place too many of those crabs in one subset and too few in the other. That imbalance can affect both the inferential and predictive parts of the workflow.

A more careful split could stratify the data by an important categorical regressor, such as female crab colour, or by a coarser grouping of the response variable. Other strategies include repeated random splits, cross-validation, or bootstrap-based assessment. These approaches can reduce dependence on a single split and are widely used in predictive modelling and model validation; see Hastie et al. (2009), Kuhn and Johnson (2013), and Harrell (2015) for broader discussions. The main chapter keeps a single 50/50 split so that the Generalized Poisson regression workflow remains transparent, and so that the results remain directly comparable with Chapter 10.

The variance-to-mean ratio computed here is a marginal summary: it ignores the regressors completely. A ratio far from one is an early warning, not a verdict. 13.6 The Generalized Poisson Regression Model

The EDA pointed to a specific problem: the training counts appear strongly overdispersed, while the expected count clearly varies with female crab size. We now build the formal probability model that can represent both features at once. Like every regression model in this book, the specification has three parts: a random component (the conditional distribution of the response), a systematic component (how the regressors enter), and a link function (how the two are connected). The first is the genuinely new piece; the second and third coincide with those of Section 10.6.

13.6.1 The Random Component

For the \(i\)th observation (\(i = 1, \ldots, n\)), let \(Y_i\) be the count response (for our case study, the number of satellite males) and let \(x_{i,1}, \ldots, x_{i,k}\) be its regressors. Conditional on those regressors, we model

\[ Y_i \mid x_{i,1}, \ldots, x_{i,k} \sim \operatorname{GPO}(\lambda_i, \theta), \tag{13.1}\]

where \(\operatorname{GPO}(\lambda_i, \theta)\) denotes the Generalized Poisson distribution with parameters \(\lambda_i > 0\) and \(\theta \in (-1, 1)\).

Definition of the Generalized Poisson distribution

A discrete random variable \(Y\) follows a Generalized Poisson distribution with parameters \(\lambda > 0\) and \(\theta \in (-1, 1)\), written \(Y \sim \operatorname{GPO}(\lambda, \theta)\), if its probability mass function (PMF) is

\[ p_Y(y; \lambda, \theta) \;=\; \frac{\lambda \, (\lambda + \theta y)^{y - 1} \, \exp\!\big(-(\lambda + \theta y)\big)}{y!}, \qquad y = 0, 1, 2, \ldots \tag{13.2}\]

(Consul and Jain 1973). Here \(\lambda\) is a rate-like parameter and \(\theta\) is the dispersion parameter. When \(\theta < 0\), the support is truncated so that \(\lambda + \theta y\) stays positive; the truncation only bites at counts far in the upper tail for the parameter values we will encounter (Consul 1989).

The mean and variance of \(Y\) are

\[ \mathbb{E}(Y) = \frac{\lambda}{1 - \theta}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \frac{\lambda}{(1 - \theta)^3}. \]

So the ratio of variance to mean is

\[ \frac{\operatorname{Var}(Y)}{\mathbb{E}(Y)} = \frac{1}{(1 - \theta)^2}: \]

equal to \(1\) when \(\theta = 0\) (which recovers the Poisson distribution), greater than \(1\) when \(\theta > 0\) (overdispersion), and smaller than \(1\) when \(\theta < 0\) (underdispersion). One useful special value for later diagnostics is the probability of a zero count, \(p_Y(0; \lambda, \theta) = \exp(-\lambda)\).

The same parameterization appears in Section D.5 in the distributional appendix.

Two properties of this random component drive everything that follows. First, the Poisson distribution is nested inside the GP family at \(\theta = 0\), which will let us formally compare the two models. Second, \(\theta\) converts directly into a variance multiplier: for example, \(\theta = 0.5\) implies a variance \(1 / (1 - 0.5)^2 = 4\) times the mean, while \(\theta = -0.5\) implies a variance \(0.44\) times the mean. We assume a single dispersion parameter \(\theta\) shared by all observations, while each observation keeps its own \(\lambda_i\).

13.6.2 The Systematic Component

As in Section 10.6, the regressors enter through a linear predictor

\[ \eta_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i,1} + \beta_2 x_{i,2} + \cdots + \beta_k x_{i,k} = \mathbf{x}_i^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}, \tag{13.3}\]

where \(\mathbf{x}_i = (1, x_{i,1}, \ldots, x_{i,k})^\top\) collects the intercept and the \(k\) regressors of observation \(i\), and \(\boldsymbol{\beta} = (\beta_0, \beta_1, \ldots, \beta_k)^\top\) is the coefficient vector. Linearity here means linearity in the coefficients; the expected count itself will not be a straight-line function of the regressors.

For the running case study we start with the same simple specification as Chapter 10: the female crab’s shell width only. The extended specification — width, weight, colour, and spine condition — is deliberately the same as the extended model in Section 10.9, so that any change in conclusions can be attributed to the random component rather than to a different set of regressors.

13.6.3 The Log Link Function

Writing \(\mu_i = \mathbb{E}(Y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i)\) for the conditional expected count, we connect the mean to the linear predictor with the same log link as Chapter 10:

\[ \log(\mu_i) = \eta_i \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \mu_i = \exp(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}). \tag{13.4}\]

The log link keeps the expected count positive for any value of the linear predictor and places the coefficients on the log expected-count scale; exponentiating a coefficient therefore gives a multiplicative change in the expected count, exactly as in Chapter 10.

Combining the link with the GP mean, \(\mu_i = \lambda_i / (1 - \theta)\), gives the rate-like parameter that enters the PMF:

\[ \lambda_i = (1 - \theta)\,\mu_i = (1 - \theta)\exp(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}). \tag{13.5}\]

The regression thus carries two sets of unknowns: the coefficient vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\), which moves the expected count, and the scalar dispersion parameter \(\theta \in (-1, 1)\), which governs how far the variance is from the mean.

Heads-up: a GLM-like model that is not a Poisson GLM!

Chapter 10’s Poisson regression is a classical generalized linear model: its Poisson random component belongs to the one-parameter exponential family, and its coefficients can be estimated by Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS). The Generalized Poisson model looks similar — same linear predictor, same log link — but its random component carries the second parameter \(\theta\), which places it outside that special case. It is best understood as a joint maximum likelihood problem in \((\boldsymbol{\beta}, \theta)\): in R we will fit it with {VGAM}’s vector generalized linear model machinery (vglm() with a GP family), and in Python we will maximize the GP log-likelihood directly. The estimation section makes the likelihood — and where the standard errors come from — precise.

13.7 Estimation

The model of Section 13.6 has two sets of unknowns: the coefficient vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta} = (\beta_0, \beta_1, \ldots, \beta_k)^\top\) and the dispersion parameter \(\theta \in (-1, 1)\). We estimate them together by maximum likelihood, conditioning on the observed values of the regressors, and using the training data only.

Under conditional independence of the responses given the regressors, the log-likelihood follows directly from the GP probability mass function (Equation 13.2) with \(\lambda_i = (1 - \theta)\exp(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \boldsymbol{\beta})\) (see Equation 13.5):

\[ \ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Big[ \log \lambda_i + (y_i - 1)\log\big(\lambda_i + \theta\, y_i\big) - \big(\lambda_i + \theta\, y_i\big) - \log(y_i!) \Big], \qquad \lambda_i = (1 - \theta)\exp\big(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}\big). \tag{13.6}\]

Unlike ordinary least squares, and unlike the Poisson GLM of Section 10.6 whose coefficients admit an efficient iteratively reweighted least squares algorithm, this log-likelihood has no closed-form maximizer: the estimators \((\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \hat{\theta})\) are the numerical solution of the \((k + 2)\)-dimensional score system. In practice, both of our toolchains maximize \(\ell\) by iterative numerical optimization over \((\boldsymbol{\beta}, \theta)\) jointly.

Uncertainty quantification is standard likelihood machinery. The variance–covariance matrix of the estimates is approximated by the inverse of the information matrix evaluated at the maximum,

\[ \operatorname{Var}\big((\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \hat{\theta})\big) \;\approx\; \big[\mathcal{I}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \hat{\theta})\big]^{-1}, \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j) = \sqrt{\big[\mathcal{I}^{-1}\big]_{jj}}, \tag{13.7}\]

and a large-sample Wald 95% confidence interval for a coefficient is \(\hat{\beta}_j \pm 1.96\,\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j)\), with the same construction applying to \(\theta\).

Two estimation subtleties are worth naming before the code.

Heads-up on expected versus observed information!

There are two ways to compute the information matrix in Equation 13.7: the expected (Fisher) information and the observed information (the negative Hessian of the log-likelihood at the maximum). Both are valid large-sample approximations, but they are not numerically identical in finite samples. VGAM::vglm() reports standard errors from the expected information, while the Python class below obtains them from the observed information returned by the numerical optimizer. Expect small differences between the two columns of output — both this chapter and the data agree that the substantive conclusions are unchanged.

The second subtlety concerns the comparison we will lean on most: the GP model against its Poisson special case. That test is a regular one, and it is worth saying why explicitly, because several later chapters in this book face non-regular nesting.

Heads-up on the Poisson-vs-GP comparison being regular!

Because \(\theta = 0\) lies inside the parameter space \((-1, 1)\) — not on its boundary — the Poisson model is nested in the GP model under ordinary regularity conditions. The classical \(\chi^2_1\) asymptotics therefore apply to the likelihood-ratio test of \(H_0: \theta = 0\) performed later in this chapter; no mixture-of-chi-squared correction is needed (contrast this with the zero-inflation probability boundary problem of Chapter 12).

13.9.1 Testing the Need for the Dispersion Parameter

The first check is targeted: is \(\theta\) doing real work? Because the Poisson model is exactly the \(\theta = 0\) member of the GP family, we can test \(H_0: \theta = 0\) with a likelihood-ratio test. As flagged in Section 13.7, \(\theta = 0\) is an interior point of \((-1, 1)\), so the classical \(\chi^2_1\) reference applies. The statistic is

\[ lr = 2\,\big[\ell_{\text{GP}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \hat{\theta}) - \ell_{\text{Poisson}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{P}})\big] = 2 \times \big[ --177.71 - \big(-217.95\big) \big] = 80.48 , \]

which is overwhelmingly significant (\(p \approx 2.9e-19\)). The dispersion parameter is not a technical nicety here; the data reject the Poisson restriction decisively.

13.9.2 The Variance Structure: Pearson Statistic

The Pearson statistic uses the residuals standardized by the GP variance function, \((y_i - \hat\mu_i)/\sqrt{\hat\mu_i/(1-\hat\theta)^2}\), not by the Poisson \(\sqrt{\hat\mu_i}\):

\[ X^2_P = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{(y_i - \hat\mu_i)^2}{\hat\mu_i/(1-\hat\theta)^2} = 62.35. \tag{13.10}\]

Against a \(\chi^2_{83}\) reference (86 training observations minus three fitted parameters), \(X^2_P / 83 = 0.75\) with an upper-tail \(p\)-value of 0.956 — there is no evidence of residual over- or under-dispersion left in the mean-standardized residuals. The contrast is stark: the same-split Classical Poisson fit — for which the Pearson variance function is \(\mu_i\) — produces \(X^2_P/84 = 3.01\), about three times too large (the failure documented in Table 10.38 of Chapter 10). The GP variance function is doing exactly what it was designed to do.

13.9.4 Residual Deviance against the Saturated GP Model

Goodness of fit is often summarized by the residual deviance: twice the log-likelihood gap between the fitted model and a saturated model that reproduces every observation. For the GP distribution the saturated fit of observation \(i\) (when \(y_i > 0\)) attains \(\max_\lambda \log p_Y(y_i; \lambda, \hat\theta)\) at

\[ \hat\lambda_i^{(sat)} = \frac{y_i(1-\hat\theta) + \sqrt{\,y_i^2(1-\hat\theta)^2 + 4\hat\theta\, y_i\,}}{2}, \tag{13.11}\]

obtained by solving \(\partial \log p_Y / \partial \lambda = 0\); for \(y_i = 0\) the log-likelihood supremum is \(0\). The resulting deviance is

\[ D = 2 \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big[\ell_i^{\text{sat}} - \hat\ell_i\big] = 121.11 \]

on \(86 - 3 = 83\) degrees of freedom, a ratio of 1.46; the same-split Poisson value was 266.58. The deviance falls by more than half once the dispersion parameter enters.

Heads-up on the deviance chi-squared approximation!

Interpreting \(D\) as \(\chi^2_{83}\) is an approximation that is trustworthy when expected counts are at least moderately large. Here many fitted expected counts are small (34% of the training crabs have \(\hat\mu_i < 2\)), so the nominal \(p\)-value of 0.004 overstates the evidence of misfit. That is why this chapter weights the Pearson check (which standardizes each observation directly) and the zero-count check (which probes a specific, interpretable feature) more heavily than the deviance — and why the residual deviance remains useful mainly as a relative number, on which the GP model improves the Poisson model by a factor of 2.2.

13.9.5 The Zero-Count Check

The EDA recorded 34 zero counts among the 86 training crabs. Under the fitted GP model the probability of a zero for observation \(i\) is \(p_Y(0; \hat\lambda_i, \hat\theta) = \exp(-\hat\lambda_i)\) (see the definition in Section 13.6.1), so the model expects

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \exp(-\hat\lambda_i) = 27.4 \]

zero nests — versus 9 under the same-split Poisson fit. The GP model recovers most of the Poisson model’s shortfall, but still under-predicts zeros by about 7 crabs. This is the diagnostic fingerprint worth remembering: a dispersion parameter widens the whole distribution symmetrically around its mean shape, whereas a structural zero surplus is targeted directly by the mixture models of Chapter 12 (ZIP) and hurdle models. Both remedies are legitimate; which one fits a given dataset is an empirical question.

13.11 Interpreting the Parameter Estimates

The extended fit of Section 13.10 gives eight regression coefficients and one dispersion parameter. The coefficient interpretation follows Chapter 10’s log-link logic exactly: on the log scale a coefficient changes the log expected count additively, so its exponential is a multiplicative expected-count ratio. What the Generalized Poisson layer changes is how much uncertainty we attach to those ratios — and, through \(\theta\), how we describe the variability a given profile should expect.

13.11.1 Interpreting the Continuous Regressors

  • Shell width (\(\hat\beta_1 = 0.078\)). Holding the other regressors fixed, a one-centimetre larger carapace is associated with an expected satellite count multiplied by \(e^{\hat\beta_1} = 1.081\), a Wald 95% interval of (0.88, 1.33) for that ratio. The point estimate is positive — larger crabs attract more satellites — but the interval includes \(1\), so the training fit alone does not separate the width effect from zero once the other regressors share the credit.
  • Weight (\(\hat\beta_2 = 0.568\)). Holding the other regressors fixed, one additional kilogram is associated with an expected count multiplied by \(e^{\hat\beta_2} = 1.77\), with interval (0.9, 3.48). One kilogram is a large step relative to the observed range (1.2–5.2 kg), so it is also instructive to rescale: per 0.1 kg, the ratio is \(e^{\hat\beta_2/10} = 1.058\).

Heads-up on interpreting width and weight together!

In the training data the correlation between width_cm and weight_kg is 0.87 — the two body-size measures largely carry the same information. “Holding the other fixed” is then a mathematical operation, not a description of a crab one is likely to see: a crab cannot easily gain a kilogram at fixed width. The individual width and weight intervals overlap substantially because the two regressors share their explanatory power; joint interpretation of the size signal (both coefficients positive, their linear combination tightly positive) is safer than strong claims about either coefficient on its own.

13.11.2 Interpreting the Categorical Regressors

For the categorical regressors, every coefficient is a contrast with the reference category (Light for colour, Both good for spine condition), so an exponentiated coefficient reads as the expected-count ratio of the named category relative to its reference, other regressors held fixed.

  • Colour. Relative to a Light female of the same size and spine condition: Medium light \(e^{\hat\beta_3} = 0.75\) (95% interval (0.34, 1.64)), Medium dark \(e^{\hat\beta_4} = 0.7\) ((0.29, 1.7)), and Dark \(e^{\hat\beta_5} = 0.3\) ((0.09, 0.97)). The estimates decrease monotonically: the point estimate for a Dark crab is about 70% fewer expected satellites than an otherwise identical Light crab, and its interval sits almost entirely below one. This direction matches the EDA gradient (Table 13.9).
  • Spine condition. Relative to Both good: One worn or broken \(e^{\hat\beta_6} = 1.19\) ((0.41, 3.47)) and Both worn or broken \(e^{\hat\beta_7} = 1.72\) ((0.93, 3.16)). Both point estimates are above one, but both intervals comfortably include one; the small One worn or broken group (7 training crabs) has a particularly wide interval, as flagged in the EDA.

13.11.3 Interpreting the Dispersion Parameter

The dispersion parameter deserves interpretation of its own, because it is the reason this chapter exists. With \(\hat\theta = 0.492\), any crab profile with fitted expected count \(\hat\mu_i\) carries

\[ \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(Y_i) = \frac{\hat\mu_i}{(1 - \hat\theta)^2} = 3.88\,\hat\mu_i, \qquad \widehat{\operatorname{SD}}(Y_i) = \frac{\sqrt{\hat\mu_i}}{1 - \hat\theta} = 1.97\,\sqrt{\hat\mu_i}. \tag{13.13}\]

A concrete calibration makes the meaning tangible. For a profile with \(\hat\mu_i = 3\): the GP model says counts fluctuate with variance 11.6 (SD \(3.4\)), whereas a Poisson model with the same mean says variance \(3\) (SD \(1.73\)). The GP model also implies \(\Pr(Y_i = 0) = \exp(-(1 - \hat\theta)\hat\mu_i) = 0.218\) — versus 0.05 under the Poisson model at the same mean. The dispersion parameter thus changes not only the widths of confidence intervals but the shape of every prediction this model makes: it more than quadruples the implied chance of an empty nest at this mean.

Tip: reporting theta alongside the coefficients!

A one-line convention that travels well into reports and papers: “fitted GP dispersion \(\hat\theta = 0.49\) (95% Wald interval 0.37 to 0.61, implying a variance 3.9 times the mean.” Readers then see both the parameter and its consequence on a familiar scale. Reporting \(\hat\theta\) alone is opaque; reporting only the ratio hides which side of the Poisson model the data landed on.

What changed relative to Chapter 10 is almost entirely how sure we are, not which way the associations point. Every statement above is still a training-stage statement: Section 13.12 turns to the predictive inquiry (held-out accuracy), and Section 13.13 will restate the inferential claims after refitting on the untouched testing set.

13.12 Prediction with Generalized Poisson Regression

Prediction proceeds exactly as in Section 10.10: form the linear predictor for a new profile, exponentiate to the expected-count scale, and — the GP addition — attach the fitted dispersion parameter. For a new female crab with regressor row vector \(\mathbf{x}_{\operatorname{new}}^\top\) (intercept plus the seven extended-model regressors),

\[ \hat\mu_{\operatorname{new}} = \exp\big(\mathbf{x}_{\operatorname{new}}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\big), \qquad \hat\lambda_{\operatorname{new}} = (1 - \hat\theta)\,\hat\mu_{\operatorname{new}}, \qquad Y_{\operatorname{new}} \mid \mathbf{x}_{\operatorname{new}} \sim \operatorname{GPO}\big(\hat\lambda_{\operatorname{new}}, \hat\theta\big). \tag{13.14}\]

The predicted value \(\hat\mu_{\operatorname{new}}\) is an expected count, not a rounded count: it can be fractional but must be positive. What the GP layer adds relative to Chapter 10 is an honest predictive spread — predictive SD \(\sqrt{\hat\mu_{\operatorname{new}}/(1-\hat\theta)^2}\) — and it makes the whole satellite-count distribution available for the profile: its zero probability is \(\exp(-\hat\lambda_{\operatorname{new}})\), its tail probabilities follow from the PMF, and so on.

13.12.1 A Manual Prediction, First

Before delegating to software, walk through one prediction by hand. The first testing observation is a Medium light, Both worn or broken female of width 28.3 cm and weight 3.05 kg (observed count: 8). Its linear predictor is

\[ \hat\eta = \underbrace{-2.591}_{\hat\beta_0} + \underbrace{0.0782}_{\hat\beta_1}(28.3) + \underbrace{0.5683}_{\hat\beta_2}(3.05) + \underbrace{(-0.2893)}_{\hat\beta_3}(1) + \underbrace{0.5399}_{\hat\beta_7}(1) = 1.607, \]

so \(\hat\mu_1 = \exp(1.607) = 4.988\) and \(\hat\lambda_1 = (1 - 0.4923) \times 4.988 = 2.532\). Under the fitted GP, \(\Pr(Y_1 = 0) = \exp(-\hat\lambda_1) = 0.079\) — where a Poisson model with the same mean would say 0.007 — and the predictive standard deviation is \(\sqrt{4.99 \times 3.88} \approx 4.4\) rather than the Poisson \(\sqrt{4.99} \approx 2.23\). The software calls below reproduce exactly this arithmetic.

Heads-up on predicting with a dispersion parameter!

A Poisson prediction collapses to its mean: once \(\hat\mu_{\operatorname{new}}\) is computed, the whole predictive distribution is fixed. A GP prediction is a genuine two-parameter distribution: \(\hat\mu_{\operatorname{new}}\) describes the location and \(\hat\theta\) describes the spread. When \(\hat\theta \approx 0.5\), the predictive SD is roughly \(1/(1-\hat\theta) \approx 1.97\) times the Poisson one at the same mean — so GP prediction intervals for individual counts are roughly twice as wide as the Poisson equivalents. Metrics that score the whole predictive distribution (the negative log predictive mass, the predictive deviance) will react to this honest widening; mean-only metrics (ME, MAE, RMSE) will not. Both families of metrics appear in Section 13.13.

13.14 Storytelling

The storytelling stage translates the full Generalized Poisson workflow into a message for an applied audience. Imagine presenting to marine biologists studying horseshoe crab reproduction. They care about two distinct things, and the story keeps them separate:

  • Inferential: which female characteristics are associated with the expected number of satellite males, and how certain are we?
  • Predictive: do those characteristics help predict satellite counts for crabs we have not measured?

The journey to the answer matters as much as the answer. Chapter 10’s Classical Poisson model failed these data: the conditional variance was roughly three times the mean, expected zeros were less than a third of what was observed, and its residual deviance was far above its degrees of freedom. This chapter’s candidate — Generalized Poisson regression — adds exactly one parameter aimed at exactly those failures. The training-stage audit showed the repair working: the Pearson statistic with the GP variance function is in line with its degrees of freedom, the expected zero count moved from 9 to 27.4 (against 34 observed), and the likelihood-ratio test formalized the need for the dispersion parameter.

From the inferential point of view, the motivating question was:

How is the expected number of satellite males associated with female crab width, weight, colour, and spine condition?

Two findings carry the answer. First, the dispersion itself is a finding: on the testing-set refit, \(\hat\theta = 0.51\) per Table 13.25, the counts vary about \(4.2\) times as much as a Poisson model with the same mean would allow, and the test of \(H_0: \theta = 0\) is decisive (\(p \approx 1.1e-20\)). Any scientific statement about these crabs that ignores this dispersion is built on an unsound uncertainty floor.

Second, within the proper uncertainty the evidence concentrates on colour. Relative to Light females, Dark females are estimated to have 70% fewer expected satellite males (95% interval for the ratio from 0.09 to 1), and Medium dark points the same way with a wider interval. The body-size variables that dominate the raw plots do not carry a statistically decisive adjusted effect at this sample size — an honest disappointment that the GP model states plainly precisely because its standard errors are calibrated. Stakeholders should hear that sentence whole: the association story is about colour; the size-gradient visible in exploratory plots is not, at these sample sizes, distinguishable from chance under a model whose uncertainty we trust. Chapter 10’s Poisson-based refit read slightly more generously Section 10.12; the difference is not new data but correctly sized intervals.

From the predictive point of view, the motivating question was:

How well can the available regressors be used to predict the expected number of satellite males for a female crab?

Plainly: the regressors are not reliable point predictors on held-out crabs. The extended GP model’s held-out MAE is 2.53 satellites against 2.5 for a rule that always predicts the training average of 2.85; RMSE tells the same story (3.29 against 3.196). Only the distribution-aware negative log predictive mass tilts marginally toward the extended model (2.255 against 2.275), reflecting the better-calibrated spread rather than better locations.

The responsible story, then, is not “the model explains satellite counts”. It is:

Satellite counts are strongly overdispersed — a stable, tested property of these data, present on data the model never saw. After adjusting honestly for this dispersion, the clearest association is darker colour with fewer satellites; the observed body-size gradient is compatible with a wide range of adjusted effects, including none. Used predictively, the crab characteristics give an honest picture of how uncertain any single crab’s count remains, rather than an accurate point forecast.

That is a usable scientific message, and it is only available because the model was allowed to say how variable the world is. The Negative Binomial and Zero-Inflated Poisson chapters show two alternative routes to overdispersion and zero surplus (variance quadratic in the mean; a two-component zero process); the exercises compare them directly against what was done here.

13.15 Chapter Summary

This chapter presented Generalized Poisson (GP) regression as the book’s answer to count data that violate equidispersion in either direction. The running horseshoe crab case study framed the two guiding inquiries summarized in Table 13.31.

The model itself has three parts (Section 13.6): a random component \(Y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i \sim \operatorname{GPO}(\lambda_i, \theta)\) with mean \(\mu_i = \lambda_i/(1-\theta)\) and variance \(\mu_i / (1-\theta)^2\); the systematic component \(\eta_i = \mathbf{x}_i^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}\); and the log link \(\log \mu_i = \eta_i\), which ties the rate-like parameter to the regressors through \(\lambda_i = (1-\theta)\exp(\eta_i)\). The dispersion parameter \(\theta \in (-1, 1)\) carries the meaning: \(\theta = 0\) nests the Classical Poisson model as an interior point, \(\theta > 0\) inflates the variance above the mean, and \(\theta < 0\) compresses it below the mean. The Chapter 13 distributional summary in Section D.5 collects the distribution’s properties independently of any regression.

Estimation is joint maximum likelihood in \((\boldsymbol{\beta}, \theta)\) — there is no iteratively reweighted least squares shortcut as in a classical GLM — and uncertainty comes from the inverse information matrix, with the expected-versus-observed information difference explaining small R/Python discrepancies (Section 13.7). In R, {VGAM}’s vglm() with genpoisson1(zero = "dispind") fits the model and we decoded its dispersion row back into \(\theta\); in Python, the chapter’s own GenericLikelihoodModel subclass maximizes the log-likelihood directly, with the logistic map keeping \(\theta\) in \((-1, 1)\).

Goodness of fit ran as a full training-stage audit (Section 13.9): the likelihood-ratio test of \(\theta = 0\) (classical \(\chi^2_1\) applies because \(0\) is interior), the Pearson statistic standardized by the GP variance function, the observed-versus-fitted comparison, the residual deviance with its small-counts approximation caveat, and the GP zero-count check \(\sum_i \exp(-\hat\lambda_i)\) — the check that flagged the remaining zero surplus as the feature a dispersion parameter cannot repair on its own.

Interpretation kept Chapter 10’s grammar (Section 13.11): coefficients are additive on the log expected-count scale and multiplicative as expected-count ratios, categorical coefficients compare against reference categories, and \(\theta\) was given its own reporting convention (parameter plus its implied variance multiplier).

The results stage delivered the conclusions on held-out data (Section 13.13): inference from a testing-set refit, prediction from the training-fitted model, six executed metrics against an intercept-only GP baseline, and an honest answer to both inquiries.

Where does this model sit among the book’s count-model tools? Negative Binomial regression (Chapter 11) models overdispersion with a variance quadratic in the mean, and Zero-Inflated Poisson regression (Chapter 12) models a structural zero surplus with a mixture; both are one-direction tools. The Generalized Poisson model’s distinguishing feature is the sign of one scalar: it treats overdispersion and underdispersion symmetrically, which is why this book places it as the capstone of the mean-variance-flexible count models. The 99 B-Line coding case at the end of the chapter exercises the underdispersed side of the same machinery — where the Classical Poisson model errs in the opposite direction — and the practice exercises develop the conceptual material (the dispersion parameter’s geometry, comparison with the sibling models, and the dispersion ratio as decision quantity).

Question 13.1

True or False

The Generalized Poisson regression model is a generalization of Classical Poisson regression that is useful only for overdispersed count responses.

Answer 13.1

Correct answer: False.

Rationale:

The dispersion parameter \(\theta\) ranges over \((-1, 1)\) and controls the variance-to-mean ratio \(\frac{1}{(1-\theta)^2}\) in both directions: \(\theta > 0\) gives overdispersion (ratio above one) and \(\theta < 0\) gives underdispersion (ratio below one). The horseshoe crab analysis used the first direction; the coding case at the end of the chapter uses the second.

Question 13.2

True or False

Setting \(\theta = 0\) in the Generalized Poisson probability mass function gives back the Poisson probability mass function exactly.

Answer 13.2

Click here to reveal the answer!

Correct answer: True.

Rationale:

Substituting \(\theta = 0\) into Equation 13.2 gives

\[ p_Y(y; \lambda, 0) = \frac{\lambda (\lambda + 0 \cdot y)^{y-1} e^{-(\lambda + 0 \cdot y)}}{y!} = \frac{\lambda \cdot \lambda^{y-1} e^{-\lambda}}{y!} = \frac{\lambda^y e^{-\lambda}}{y!}, \]

the Poisson PMF with mean \(\lambda\). This exact nesting — at an interior point of the parameter space — is what makes the likelihood-ratio test of \(H_0: \theta = 0\) a plain \(\chi^2_1\) test.

Question 13.4

Open-ended Question

Why is the estimation of a Generalized Poisson regression not an ordinary iteratively reweighted least squares problem like the Poisson GLM of Chapter 10?

Answer 13.4

Click here to reveal the answer!

IRLS works because the Poisson GLM’s random component belongs to the one-parameter exponential family: the mean function alone determines the variance, so coefficients can be updated with weights derived only from \(\mu_i\). The GP random component carries the additional parameter \(\theta\), so the variance \(\mu_i/(1-\theta)^2\) is what the likelihood says it is only when \(\theta\) is optimized jointly with \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\). Estimation is therefore a \((k+2)\)-dimensional maximum likelihood problem — handled in R by {VGAM}’s vector GLM machinery with Fisher scoring, and in Python by direct numerical maximization of Equation 13.6.

Question 13.5

Open-ended Question

Distinguish the inferential roles of \(\exp(\hat\beta_j)\) and \(\hat\theta\) in a fitted GP model: which aspects of the response distribution does each one summarize, and does either one depend on the units of the regressors?

Answer 13.5

Click here to reveal the answer!
  • \(\exp(\hat\beta_j)\) is an expected-count ratio: the multiplicative change in the conditional mean per unit increase of regressor \(j\), holding the other regressors fixed. It depends on the regressor’s units (per cm, per kg, category-vs-reference).
  • \(\hat\theta\) describes the conditional spread relative to the mean, through the ratio \(1/(1-\hat\theta)^2\). It is unit-free: it reflects heterogeneity or regularity in the data-generating process, not a per-unit change.

So the coefficient vector locates the distribution, and the dispersion parameter shapes it.

Question 13.6

Open-ended Question

The likelihood-ratio test of \(H_0: \theta = 0\) against \(H_1: \theta \neq 0\) was calibrated against \(\chi^2_1\) in this chapter, while the comparison between Poisson and Zero-Inflated Poisson in Chapter 12 required more care. Where does the difference come from?

Answer 13.6

Click here to reveal the answer!

It comes from where the null value sits in the parameter space. For GP, \(\theta = 0\) lies strictly inside the legal range \((-1, 1)\), so the usual regularity conditions for the likelihood-ratio test hold and \(\chi^2_1\) applies. For ZIP, the null \(\pi = 0\) (no zero-inflated component) lies on the boundary of \([0, 1)\), which breaks the standard conditions; the asymptotic reference is a mixture of chi-squared distributions rather than a plain \(\chi^2\). Same machinery, different geometry.

Answer 13.8

Click here to reveal the answer!
  • Negative Binomial: \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_i) = \mu_i + \mu_i^2/\kappa\) with \(\kappa > 0\) — a quadratic variance function; the relative overdispersion shrinks as the mean grows.
  • Generalized Poisson: \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_i) = \mu_i/(1-\theta)^2\) — a constant proportional multiplier; the variance-to-mean ratio is the same at every mean level.

The GP model is the only option of the two for underdispersed counts: the Negative Binomial variance can never fall below the mean, while \(\theta < 0\) gives variance below the mean.

Question 13.9

Open-ended Question

One of the chapter’s goodness-of-fit checks compared the observed number of zero counts (34 in the training data) against the model expectation \(\sum_i \exp(-\hat\lambda_i)\). Why does this check describe something a dispersion parameter cannot repair by itself, and which model family from Chapter 12 addresses precisely that failure?

Answer 13.9

Click here to reveal the answer!

The dispersion parameter rescales the whole distribution proportionally: it decides how spread out counts are around the mean at every level of the mean. A structural surplus at the single point \(y = 0\) is a local distortion (some nests attract no satellites at all for structural reasons). Neither the GP ratio nor the NB variance can bend one point of the PMF independently — that is exactly what a mixture does: the zero-inflated (or hurdle) family models zero counts as coming from an extra process with its own probability, which is why those models were the subject of Chapter 12.

Question 13.10

Open-ended Question

In the held-out metric table of Table 13.28, the extended model and the training-mean baseline nearly tied on MAE and RMSE, but disagreed more clearly on the mean negative log predictive mass. Explain mechanistically why the dispersion parameter can change one family of metrics without changing the other.

Answer 13.10

Click here to reveal the answer!

ME, MAE, and RMSE score only the predicted mean \(\hat\mu_i\) against the observed count \(y_i\); neither \(\theta\) nor the rest of the predictive distribution enters. The mean GP deviance and the mean negative log predictive mass evaluate \(\log p_Y(y_i; \hat\lambda_i, \hat\theta)\), which depends explicitly on \(\theta\). A larger fitted spread raises the predictive probability of counts far from the mean and lowers it near the centre, so a model whose dispersion is well calibrated can look identical on mean-only metrics yet better (or worse) on mass-based metrics.

The simple model conditions the arrival counts on rain only. For rush-hour window \(i\), let \(r_{i}\) be the binary indicator that it is raining (\(r_{i}=1\) if rain, \(0\) otherwise):

(i) Underdispersion: confirmed. The testing-scale dispersion estimate \(\hat\theta = -0.2859\) (standard error 0.0882; 95% interval -0.459 to -0.113, entirely below zero) implies a conditional variance-to-mean ratio of 0.605, and the likelihood-ratio test against the nested Poisson scale gives \(\chi^2_1 = 11.21\), \(p = 8.13\times 10^{-4}\). Rush-hour arrivals on this corridor are materially more regular than a Poisson model allows, with or without conditioning on conditions — the schedule works.

(ii) Associations: weak and direction-consistent. Rainy windows are associated with an expected-count ratio of 0.943 (95% Wald interval 0.88 to 1.011; \(p = 0.101\)): a suggestion of roughly 6% fewer arrivals in rain, not significant at the 5% level given the day of the week. Standard errors. The GP standard errors on the testing refit are 78% of the Classical Poisson ones — the mirror image of the crab analysis, where ignoring overdispersion had understated uncertainty. Briefing — 99 B-Line rush-hour reliability review.

Across 200 weekday rush-hour windows, the 99 B-Line delivered about 20 bus arrivals per hour at the central stop, and — the review’s headline — those arrivals were significantly more regular than random scheduling would produce. After modelling the counts with a Generalized Poisson regression, the implied variance of hourly arrivals is roughly 60% of what the standard Poisson benchmark would assign at the same mean (testing-scale ratio 0.6; \(p \approx 8.1\times 10^{-4}\) on held-out data). Peak service on the corridor is tight, as the dispatch protocol intends.

Confidence. The regularity finding is robust: it appears in both halves of the data, at nearly identical strength, and survives out-of-sample refitting. The secondary findings are weaker. Rainy rush hours show slightly fewer arrivals — about 6% fewer per hour — but the association does not reach conventional significance on the held-out refit, so it should be treated as suggestive rather than established. Day-to-day differences were jointly detectable in the model-development data but were too small individually to be decision-relevant, and the extra weekday parameters did not improve predictions on new windows.

Recommendations and limits. For reliability KPIs, schedule-adherence dashboards, and passenger-information variance margins, the uncertainty bands calibrated to a Poisson assumption are too wide — roughly a fifth too wide here — so service targets set on those bands would be needlessly conservative. Nothing here licenses causal claims: the rain pattern reflects conditions concurrent with rain (traffic, boarding times, seasonal timetables), not the effect of weather alone; and the analysis covers rush-hour behaviour at one corridor — other routes, or off-peak windows where dispatch control is looser, could plausibly be overdispersed and would need their own fit.
